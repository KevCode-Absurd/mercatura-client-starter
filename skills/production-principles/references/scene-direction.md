# Scene direction and the audience's change

Develop the idea into a direction record before the execution prompt: audience purpose/feeling translated into visible evidence, approved scene/action, camera/light, reference roles, fixed/open choices and review criteria. Use [Prompt decisions](prompt-decisions.md) to keep user intent and proposals distinct, then translate into actual route controls without changing the approved message. A still depicts one state; video needs a feasible journey and timing. A helper organizes those decisions rather than replacing the director; refinement retains what worked instead of redesigning every layer.

Adapted from the user-supplied “Director’s Cut: The Decisions That Shape a Scene” lesson and three café stills (2026-10-09). These are examples of decisions, not required scenes, casting, lengths or a request to generate the exercise. The brand's world, approved script, product facts and format remain authoritative.

## Decide what the viewer understands

Write one sentence before planning coverage: “At first the viewer thinks [starting interpretation]; after [visible evidence], they understand [new interpretation].” A simple demonstration can move from uncertainty about a mechanism to understanding how it works; it does not need a dramatic twist. Name the evidence that earns the change. An attractive close-up alone does not establish it.

For D2C, a turn might reveal that a seemingly bulky bag folds flat, or that a jacket's apparently decorative pocket has a documented function. Show only capabilities in the product facts. A generated reaction, customer-looking presenter or staged before/after does not prove efficacy, satisfaction or conversion. The approved words stay unchanged; directing improves how their argument is seen.

## Give each shot a purpose and one event

For a borrowed story device, read [Memorable visual ideas](memorable-visual-ideas.md): carry competing agendas, disproportionate effort, a reveal/reversal or callback into an original format rather than requiring sitcom coverage. Make the tension and earned payoff legible with the minimum useful beats; an ad's product role remains supported by its facts. A staged technical failure, laugh or reaction is storytelling, not product proof. Preserve actual state changes and fit speech/action into the chosen crop and duration rather than copying an example's timestamps.

Plan shots by the information they add. Wide context, medium action, detail and reaction are useful options, not a required order. Keep only coverage that establishes a necessary fact, changes an interpretation or makes a demonstrated action legible. Static ads can borrow this hierarchy within one composition; they do not inherit video timing.

| Plan field | Decision |
|---|---|
| Time and purpose | Start/end, what the viewer knows before and learns after |
| Frame and attention | Crop, height, angle, gaze; the first thing the eye should find |
| Visible event | One main observable action with a feasible start and finish |
| Continuity | Character, wardrobe, product/prop count and state, position, light sources |
| Cut or hold | The visible cue that permits a cut, or the reason to stay |

Replace “looks powerful” with behavior the camera can record: sets the cup down, rests fingertips beside the envelope, then looks toward the visitor. Sequential supporting behavior may serve one event; competing operations need separate shots. Attention can move from an arriving character to a significant object and back to a reaction. Each transfer needs a visible cue and a coherent eye line.

## Leave time for the evidence to register

A detail or reaction may need a brief hold before cutting. Hold the framing with natural live behavior, not frozen footage or slowed playback. End the pause when the viewer can recognize the new information. In direct-response phone UGC, keep the established pace and use a pause only when it helps a demonstration or payoff; do not impose cinematic pacing, a twist or a reaction shot on every ad. Fit speech, action and recognition time inside the actual clip duration. Preserve whole words at joins.

## Carry state across cuts

Use a continuity record for each shot's start and finish: hand position, object count, sealed/open state, where characters stand, gaze target, wardrobe and physical light sources. A window stays in the same place in the room; its screen position may change with the camera. Keep screen direction and eye lines coherent. Change framing intentionally rather than changing the room to match a new crop. See [Camera shots](camera-shots.md) and [Video starting points](video-generation.md).

The supplied wide café still shows a held cup and another cup on a saucer. The medium still shows one cup on the table and a hand beside the envelope; the close view shows a hand supporting the cheek and crops out the envelope. These are different illustrations, not proof that cup count, hand movement or gaze connects in a finished sequence. Check whether an extra cup remains off frame or disappears, and plan the hand transition. A camera-facing gaze does not automatically read as looking at the visitor. The close-up's expression alone cannot prove that the audience understood the intended reveal.

## Review the edit, then refine one decision

Watch the complete sequence without dialogue first. Can a fresh viewer identify the approach, the important object and the intended change in interpretation? Then watch with sound to check approved speech, action sounds and timing. If the turn is unclear, identify the missing cue: context, prop visibility, gaze, expression or recognition time. Adjust that decision; a longer close-up helps only if it contains the needed evidence. Review the actual motion and joins, not just selected stills. Record the cue or pause that makes the change readable and any remaining uncertainty. Production readability is separate from measured ad performance.
