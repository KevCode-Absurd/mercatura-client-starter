# Camera shots and coverage

Adapted from the user-supplied “Camera Shots” lesson and its twelve images (2026-10-09). Their locations, casting, focal lengths and cinematic styling are examples, not default requirements. The images show framing choices; their origin and performance are unknown.

Also incorporates “The Visual Language of AI Video” and its three café stills (2026-10-09): purposeful shot scale, physically placed cameras, one main camera behaviour, source-based light and timed action. The six-second exercise and community-sharing instruction are source examples, not work to execute.

The supplied Nuova Era Shot Architect package contributes physical camera placement, meaningful variation and visible perspective consequences. Its four-prompt default, platform claims, formatting mandates and historical camera bans do not override the job's brief, output schema or authorized render count.

Use this material as examples and conditional craft rules. The user's brief, brand world and frame's job determine the actual scene and which rules matter; no example becomes a mandatory template or a preset to reproduce.

## Specify a camera decision

Choose the frame's job, then write: **shot type + exact visible boundaries + subject/action + camera position + focus/perspective + light + format-appropriate style**. Include orientation and space for copy where the approved plan requires it. A lens number is optional direction, not proof of the equipment used in a reference; camera distance and position must support the described perspective.

Shot scale, camera height and viewpoint are separate. A low angle can be wide or close. A full shot can show little or much environment. Emotional effects depend on subject scale, context and action: a low camera can emphasise a tall product or make a person small beneath a larger structure. Do not claim that an angle guarantees an emotion or conversion.

## Shot architecture: place, perspective and moment

Start with what the viewer needs to see. Choose shot distance and the camera's physical place together, then orientation, foreground, crop and focus; height and tilt serve those choices. Eye level is useful, not a failure of imagination. “From the next seat at seated height” defines more than “cinematic low angle.” Name the location, not an imaginary camera operator who might accidentally become another person in frame.

Record only the decisions that matter:

| Decision | Concrete direction |
|---|---|
| Purpose and moment | The mechanism at contact, the expression after a reveal, or the room that explains the routine |
| Physical place and distance | Across the table, beside the workbench, inside a doorway, or a supported studio position; enough space for the crop |
| Lens/perspective | Wide and near, normal conversational view, or longer lens from farther back; focal length is optional appearance direction |
| Orientation and boundary | Three-quarter, exact profile or frontal; waist-up with both hands, full product, or a named detail |
| Depth and focus | Foreground edge, subject and background; which plane carries the evidence and which may soften |
| Visible consequence | One cue proving the view: tabletop fills the lower third, ceiling visible above the subject, or foreground edge frames the distant task |

Perspective changes with camera position. A focal length changes framing from a fixed place; moving back with a longer lens to retain the crop changes near/far relationships. Do not claim a lens alone compresses space or guarantees a precise render. Numeric lens, distance and crop cues must agree; omit unsupported precision rather than combining a face-distance macro with a full-body view. On phone UGC use its own capture language, not an invented cinema lens kit.

Check the sightline: the camera position, visible side of the subject, background and light sources must coexist. A camera across the table can see the face beyond it; an exact profile from the side cannot simultaneously show a frontal view of that face. Glass and foreground frames need plausible scale, transparency and reflections. Reserve enough clear frame for the product, grip and planned words. Extreme near-lens perspective must not misrepresent product capacity, garment fit or size.

## Explore setups without losing the brief

Separate three jobs:

- **Exploration:** propose distinct photographic interpretations. When useful, change several camera choices together and label the difference. The number follows the request; four concepts is a useful option, not four automatic paid renders.
- **Matching coverage:** choose complementary views of one scene that add evidence. Preserve cast, room/light geometry and current product state; different moments need explicit state handoffs.
- **Local refinement:** retain the approved setup and change only the requested decision. A locked crop, identity sheet, turnaround, technical PDP angle or upscale does not need a diversity pass.

Before drafting alternatives, compare short labels: **shot size · perspective/lens if relevant · physical position · moment**. If labels read alike, change a meaningful choice such as distance, viewpoint, foreground relationship or focus hierarchy. Vary height only when it changes useful visible information. When the brief locks close-up distance, explore orientation, crop, background relationship or focus within that boundary instead of adding an unwanted wide shot.

Choose depth deliberately. Use deeper focus when the background explains the joke or routine; soften it when a detail is the evidence. Do not impose shallow focus on every “cinematic” shot. Overhead, floor-level, canted and aerial views are available when requested or when the scene benefits; do not re-pose the subject merely to justify one. There is no universal quota or banned focal length.

For a manually requested four-concept exploration, one portable drinkware idea could produce:

| Setup label | Distinct contribution |
|---|---|
| Detail · workbench edge · closure moment | Hand and actual closure fill the frame; useful focus depth keeps the contact readable |
| Medium · across the table · reaction | Face and held product remain visible; conversational distance and restrained foreground |
| Wide · doorway · preparation | The adult, bag and desk share a readable room; deeper focus explains the routine |
| Distant · through a clear window · departure | Longer-lens framing from farther back layers the window and room; use only if the product remains sufficiently visible for this frame's job |

These are concepts, not an obligatory sequence or product claims. Keep approved identity, wardrobe, actual product and light-source locations; describe a later moment as later rather than silently moving props. Replace a weak distant shot with a clearer view when the selling detail needs it.

Translate the selected setup into the existing prompt structure: camera place and boundary, one visible consequence, subject/action, physical light, depth and selected finish. Keep product/reference bindings and exact text sections where the workflow requires them. Four or five prose sentences, a mandatory realism slogan, extra noise or a “vibes” line are not required. For video, this still setup is the starting view; choose and time movement separately. For animation, use the same composition logic while retaining its drawn/material style rather than forcing photoreal optics or skin.

## The twelve choices

Prompt fragments describe composition; combine them with the actual product facts, approved casting, brand world and light. They are not complete prompts to render automatically.

| Shot | Exact direction | D2C job and failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Wide | Camera far enough back that the subject is small within a legible environment; name the ground, background and intended subject position | Show room scale or a product's place in a routine. A tiny product cannot also have readable label detail; pair it with a closer frame. |
| Full | Head to toe, shoes visible, room above the head and below the feet; state camera height and direction | Show clothing fit, a carried object or body movement. Avoid accidental foot cropping or distortion that misrepresents fit. |
| Medium | Name the waist-up or chest-up boundary, eye-level or other camera height, action and each hand's place | Explain, hold, use or compare a product. Keep the complete grip and mechanism visible if the demonstration needs them. |
| Close-up | Shoulders-up portrait or a clearly bounded product close view; name the dominant feature and focus | Show a reaction, application or construction. Cut background detail that competes with the subject; preserve space for the actual product when needed. |
| Extreme close-up | One named detail fills the frame; explicitly exclude a full-face or whole-object view; specify useful focus depth | Show fabric weave, stitching, a closure or visible texture. Do not invent microscopic structure or use a beauty detail as unsupported proof of efficacy. |
| Over the shoulder | Camera behind subject A; name the foreground shoulder/head edge and the sharp subject or task beyond | Show someone using the product or a dialogue relationship. Keep the foreground from blocking the selling detail. |
| Low angle | Camera below the subject, looking upward; name height, tilt and relative scale against surroundings | A hero or mechanism from below. Perspective must not falsely enlarge capacity, size or fit; a low camera does not automatically mean a dominant subject. |
| High angle | Camera above and to one side, looking diagonally down; name the visible front/side planes | Show the buyer's routine, workspace or interaction. This is not a perpendicular flat lay. |
| POV | Camera occupies the person's plausible eye position; show only hands/forearms where appropriate and what they see | Let the viewer follow opening, application or handling. Keep hands anatomically placed and free from an impossible third-person view. |
| Overhead / top down | Camera directly above, looking perpendicular to the surface; no diagonal viewpoint | Flat lay, order contents, routine kit or graphic arrangement. Do not invent bundle pieces or flatten real object height inconsistently. |
| Profile | Exact side view, facing left or right; no turn toward the camera; define crop and focus | Silhouette, garment side fit, footwear form or quiet reaction. A three-quarter face is not the requested profile. |
| Frame within a frame | Name the foreground doorway/window/other frame and the subject beyond it; state their depth and focus relationship | Show a product in a room or guide attention through a real opening. Keep the frame from hiding the product or creating an unintended border graphic. |

## Build coverage without losing continuity

For a small coverage study, a wide/context frame, medium/action frame and close/detail frame can show three useful scales. Choose only the scales the job needs; twelve shot types are a vocabulary, not a quota. Do not trigger paid batches from the lesson's exercise alone.

For an edited sequence, use [Scene direction](scene-direction.md) to define what each shot changes in the viewer's understanding. Choose the cut or brief live hold from the evidence the viewer needs to recognize, rather than adding angles for variety alone.

For new coverage derived from one still, retain and compare against the approved anchor instead of trusting a chain of changed images. Separate the new viewpoint from any intended later hand/product state. [Reference storyboards](reference-storyboards.md) covers still approval, independent animation, joins and sound.

Hold the approved identity, hair, wardrobe, actual product and variant, props, time of day, weather, light-source positions and colour treatment. Change the shot boundaries, camera position and focus deliberately. Shadows remain attached to their physical light sources; “light from the left of the image” may need translating when the camera reverses. A new viewpoint does not preserve every pixel of the old background.

Track product state and hands: sealed versus open, amount remaining, where an object rests and what the action just changed. Use tagged product views that support the new viewpoint. If the new angle reveals an undocumented back, inside or mechanism, use a supported angle instead of inventing it.

For dialogue reverses, preserve seating, eye lines, wardrobe, object positions and screen direction. A POV sequence can establish the person looking, show their plausible view, then return to the reaction. Use this only when it advances the message; it does not replace a clear demonstration.

Camera position and camera movement are different instructions. A still defines position and framing; a video plan separately defines any achievable movement. For unusual viewpoints, establish and check the opening still before paying for motion.

## Choose one camera behaviour for the shot

Position is where the camera starts; behaviour is what it does over time. Describe an achievable position relative to the subject or surface, not “a cinematic angle.” An eye-level view across a table can show face and hands, a doorway view can establish the room, and a close view near the product can make its mechanism legible. Confirm the approved starting frame actually shows what the action requires.

| Behaviour | What to specify | What it can reveal |
|---|---|---|
| Locked | Fixed position and framing, with natural subject motion | A small gesture, gaze change or precise mechanism |
| Push in | Physical move toward a named subject, start/end crop, when it begins and stops | A detail or reaction that becomes the focus |
| Pull back | Move away, end boundary, surroundings revealed | How a product or action fits its environment |
| Follow | Travel with the subject, direction, distance and framing maintained | An action across a space |
| Handheld | Small physically plausible corrections from the chosen grip | Everyday presence without hiding the demonstration |

A physical push changes perspective and parallax; a zoom changes magnification from a fixed position. Name the intended operation rather than asking for both. One main behaviour may begin after the action starts: “a small push from 4–6 seconds” is one timed move, not a request to alternate between unrelated movements. Locked is a deliberate choice. A propped UGC camera cannot travel by itself; a handheld move must leave the necessary hands available.

Keep physical light sources in their room positions across angles. Describe direction, softness and colour of the source, plus any warmer or dimmer background source. Translate source direction into the new camera view; do not move the window simply to keep it on screen left. Maintain plausible exposure, shadows and reflections during movement, with the real product colours intact.

## Fit the production format

- **Phone UGC:** arm-length or propped capture, plausible hand availability, everyday surroundings and recognisable background. Use shot boundaries, height, angle and gaze; do not copy cinematic lens vocabulary, shallow focus or camera rigs from the examples.
- **PDP and photoshoot:** choose angles that answer fit, size, construction, texture, contents or use. Keep a recognisable hero and legible selling detail. Close views supplement the whole product where needed.
- **Styled campaign:** low angles, macro, silhouettes and foreground framing can be deliberate. Product truth, brand fit and clarity still decide whether they work.
- **Upscaling:** preserve the approved camera and framing. New coverage is a different task, never a resolution-only edit.

## Check the frame

Does the requested boundary appear? Are the feet, grip or mechanism visible when required? Do position, distance, lens cue and sightline agree, with the named perspective consequence visible? Is the camera height/angle correct? Is high angle distinct from overhead, and profile distinct from three-quarter? Does the focus preserve the selling detail? Do eye lines, lighting and product state agree across coverage? Are alternatives meaningfully different within their locks? Can the viewer understand the frame's job at phone size? Keep successful choices as concrete direction, not vague “cinematic” praise.
