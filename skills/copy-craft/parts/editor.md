**The editor's read.** Read the draft through once as if aloud, and notice where attention rises and falls, where belief grows and where it leaks. Then go field by field.

**Name the colour first.** Before fixing anything, find the 1 to 3 things in the draft that are truly interesting: a fact, how it works, an odd true detail. Those are what the buyer remembers, and every fix protects them. Colour is a true detail, never a clever phrasing, and never a louder adjective.

**A field fails when,** in this order:
- **Nobody would say it, or a stranger would not understand it.** The first fault to look for. A clipped phrase with its small words dropped. A row of 1 or 2 word sentences. A clever turn that has to be decoded. A word a 13 year old does not know. A made-up name or a term from inside the business, with no explanation in the same sentence. The fix is the whole sentence a person would say to a friend. A fragment is a fault unless it is a label: a price, an amount, a guarantee term, a button.
- **It gives the buyer no reason.** A count, a spec or a feature that never says what changes for them.
- **Nothing happens in it.** A piece for a buyer who does not yet know an answer exists argues or lists where it should tell what happened to someone, or brings the product in early. A line edit cannot mend that: say so in the summary.
- **The reader can stop comfortably.** The first line does not make the second one wanted, or a question is opened and never answered.
- **The opening promises what the piece does not pay.** If a buyer who took the opening literally would feel tricked, fix the opening.
- **A benefit cannot be pictured.** "Improve", "boost", "upgrade your experience". Show the moment, the action or the result in the buyer's day. For a trust word ("trusted", "proven", "loved"): what would you point at? If the brand's material has that thing, put it there. If not, leave the line as it is.
- **It talks about the brand where the buyer's world should lead,** or it blames the reader. The old way or the usual shortcut can be the villain. The buyer's past choices cannot.
- **The words repeat the picture,** or the price, the main claim or the ask live only inside the image.
- **The last sentence of a block is a slogan.** It should be the clearest concrete sentence: the takeaway or the next step.
- **The ask says where the button is,** not what is on the other side of it.
- **A line that carries the piece (the hook, the headline, the first line) could open any ad in the category,** and the brand's material holds something only this product or this buyer's situation can say. Put that in, as a plain sentence. This is never by itself a reason to rewrite: a line that is plain, makes sense and gives a reason stays.

**Every fix has to beat the line it replaces.** Compare the 2 in place. Keep the fix only when a stranger would follow it more easily, a person would more readily say it, or it adds a reason, attention or belief. Plainer wins. A clipped line may be lengthened into a sentence. Softer and vaguer is never a fix, and neither is cleverer.
- Say what the fault costs: clarity, attention, trust or the sale. If you cannot name the cost, it is not a fault.
- "Could be shorter" is not a fault.
- A bold claim, a superlative in the seller's own voice, a big promise or a round number is not a craft fault. Whether it is proved belongs to the claims check, never to this read.
- "Most", "about", "usually" and a range are honest where no measurement exists. Never ask for a number the brand does not have.
- These are right as written: a sentence that lists 3 different facts, and a "no" inside a sentence that names what the buyer feared ("You don't need any tools to put it together.").
- If the fixed line is one nobody would say with money on the line, the fix is the fault.
- The odd, human, specific detail is usually the colour. Never cut it for sounding informal.
