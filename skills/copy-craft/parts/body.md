**The body: what the lines between the opening and the ask have to do.**

**4 things, and a piece missing 1 leaves the buyer a way out.** A problem this buyer has now. A promise only this product can make. Proof beside the claim it supports. An easy next step. They are what the body holds, not an order: the opening decides which comes first.

**The 1 interesting true thing.** Before writing, find what in the brand's material a rival could not say: a fact, how it is made, how it works, a contrast, an odd detail a customer noticed. Build the piece around it. A piece with nothing a rival could not say is correct and forgotten, and adding adjectives to it makes it louder, not better.

**Sell against what they do now.** The real rival is often the buyer's own way, or doing nothing. Show why it falls short without blaming them. The old way, the category's habit or the usual shortcut can be the villain. The buyer never is.

**Make it seen.**
- If a line cannot become a picture, a moment, an action, an object or a number, zoom in until it can. "Easy to make" becomes "You fill it, press 1 button, and the coffee is ready before the toast is".
- Take each benefit into the buyer's day: a chain of what follows from it, not a string of describing words.
- If you could only point, not explain, what would you point at? The label, the stopwatch, the review, the pan after 5 years. Point at that.
- At the moments that carry the piece (the opening, the turn, the result), let it be seen, heard or felt. Not in every line.
- A detail about the product (its taste, its shape, a time, an amount) comes from the brand's facts and nowhere else. A scene from the buyer's life comes from the research, and carries no number the research does not give ("9 days", "by week 3").
- Words go inside quotation marks only when a customer said them and they were given to you as that. The way buyers talk goes into your own sentence.

**Proof.**
- Proof proves the thing that was promised. A study of 1 result does not back a promise of another: choose the proof that answers this buyer's doubt, or make the promise the one the proof supports.
- The size of the proof matches the size of the claim. A big claim on small proof is doubted whole. A modest claim, proved, is believed.
- Proof sits in the same breath as the claim it backs, not in a block at the end. A claim a doubtful buyer would question has its backing within 1 sentence.
- Each kind of proof answers a different doubt. Other buyers: did it work for people like me? A figure: is that the rule or 1 lucky case? A demonstration: is it real? Who made it: who are you? The guarantee: what if it does not work for me? Use the kind that answers the doubt this buyer has.
- A demonstration beats a describing word. Showing that results vary is believed more than "everyone".
- A real, small flaw admitted early, with the benefit that outweighs it in the next line, makes the rest believed. For a doubtful or comparing buyer. A cold buyer gets the benefit first.
- Only what the brand's material holds. Nothing invented is ever shown as real: no figure, review, expert, study, date or count.

**No comfortable place to stop.**
- The only job of a line is to get the next one read. The first sentence is short and easy to start.
- No paragraph ends on a finished thought in the middle of the argument. Every question opened is answered, and never left hanging.
- A small yes early makes the big yes easier: true things about their situation they nod to, before anything is asked of them.
- The product does not arrive in the first line for a buyer who is not looking for it. For a buyer who knows it, it can lead.
- Answer the questions in the order the buyer would ask them.
- Cut any line a good salesperson would not say to this buyer in person.
