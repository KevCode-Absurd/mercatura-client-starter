**A headline, the words on an image, a first line.** It is a plain sentence, or a phrase a person would say, that does 2 things:
1. **It picks its reader.** The right buyer thinks "this is for me" and the wrong one moves on. It picks them by their situation or their problem, not by a label for the person.
2. **It gives them a benefit** that is specific, wanted and believable: bold up to the edge of what is true, never past it.

**The first test, before any other:** would a stranger who has never heard of the brand understand it at once, and would a person say it aloud to a friend? A line that fails is written again, whatever else it does. Clear beats clever: a clever line has to be decoded before it can be judged. A whole sentence is welcome ("Our old dog finishes his dinner now."). When the space is small, say a smaller thing. Never drop the small words to make it fit.

**The brand comes in after that:** who it talks to, its words for things, its tone. A habit of its headlines (a rhythm, a set length, a turn of phrase) is used only where the line takes it without bending. A stranger can tell from the line what kind of thing is being sold.

**What pulls.** Self-interest first, then news, then curiosity. Curiosity alone brings the wrong people: pair it with a benefit the piece pays off.

**By where the buyer is.** A strong line written for the wrong stage still fails.
- **Knows the product and wants it:** the offer and the true reason to act now, as a sentence, and nothing else.
- **Knows the product, not convinced:** the argument is which one, not whether. The proof, what only this has, or the doubt answered. The category's own terms are welcome here, each beside what it does for them.
- **Knows what they want, has not connected it to this:** the benefit said plainly, with what makes this one different in their words ("It heats evenly, so the middle doesn't burn."). Opening on the problem is 1 step too far back for this buyer.
- **Knows the problem:** name it more exactly than they can: the thing that keeps happening, not the remedy. No product in the line yet, and no technical term.
- **Does not know yet:** a moment from their day they recognise, said plainly ("I used to wake up at 3 with a sore back."). No product, no claim, no technical term.
- Between 2 stages, write for the less aware one.

**"So what?"** Take the benefit up the ladder: what it is, what it does, how that feels, the day it gives them. "Cuts prep time by a third" is level 2. "Dinner is on the table before the kids start asking" is level 4. Write at the level of their day.

**4 ways in, each a whole sentence.** When several headlines are asked for, no 2 make the same argument or lead with the same fact.
- **The plain benefit,** with its number or its way: "These shoes still grip when the pavement is wet."
- **How to** get a result without the thing they dread: "How to fry an egg without it sticking to the pan."
- **The reason why** something unexpected is true: "Why your budget falls apart in the third week of the month."
- **A customer's own words,** in quotation marks, only when they were given to you word for word as something a customer said. The way buyers talk goes into your own sentence and never inside quotation marks: a quote nobody said is an invented review.

**Check each.** Would a stranger understand it, and would a person say it? Is a clear benefit stated? Is it about 1 thing? If the answer is a list, pick 1. The headline, the points, the body and the offer all say the same 1 idea.
