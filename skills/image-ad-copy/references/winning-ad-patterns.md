# Winning image ad patterns

## What this is and how to use it

This file summarises patterns found in 589 analysed static image ads from a curated library of e-commerce ads that were saved as winners. Use it to choose a layout, decide how much copy goes on the image, and pick headline, proof and offer devices that fit the audience's awareness stage.

How to use it:

1. Decide the awareness stage first. How full the image is, the layout and what the headline says all change by stage.
2. Pick one layout archetype from section 3, then fill its slots: headline, hero visual, support copy, proof, offer, brand.
3. Compare the draft with the numbers in section 2 and the checklist in section 6.
4. Borrow structure only. Never reuse another brand's wording, claims, numbers, names or offer terms.

Honest limits:

- The library is curated. Someone judged these ads to be winners. No spend, ROAS or CTR data is attached, so nothing here is a test result.
- Counts show what is common among saved winners, not what causes performance.
- Every field was written by a vision model in a lighter "index" pass, not by a human. Stage labels are judgement calls: 398 of 589 are marked high confidence, 190 medium, 1 low.
- "On-image copy" is the model's transcription of readable text. It includes text printed on packaging and logos, so word counts run a little high and regex counts can miss stylised text.
- Only the image was analysed. Primary text, headline field and button outside the image are not in the data. A missing call to action on the image does not mean the ad had none.
- The mix leans to supplements, wellness, fashion, bedding and home, jewellery and beauty. 578 of 589 are in English. 323 distinct advertisers are named, 86 ads have no identified advertiser, and one supplement brand accounts for 34 ads (6%), another for 20.
- Every count of words describes what was saved. None is a target: how many words a line has is decided by what it has to say, as a plain sentence a person would say.

About the sample (n=589):

- Sophistication stage as labelled: stage 2 (bigger claim) 183 (31%), stage 3 (mechanism) 169 (29%), stage 5 (identity and experience) 126 (21%), stage 1 (plain claim) 58 (10%), stage 4 (improved mechanism) 45 (8%), unknown 8 (1%).
- Aspect ratio: 9:16 in 287 (49%), 1:1 in 242 (41%), 4:5 in 58 (10%).
- The analyst flags a missing call to action in 216 (37%). See the limits above: the platform button is not in the data.

## Patterns by image slot

What saved winners did, not rules. Where a pattern meets a rule of the skill or the step (every line a plain sentence or a phrase a person says, 20 added words, real proof only, no arrow that carries meaning, the brand's name never typed as a logo), the rule wins. Take the layout and the job of each slot from here, never the length or the wording of a line.

### Headline slot

**Problem question.** Problem-aware (22% of 157 carry a question mark; 0 of 48 unaware and 0 of 59 most-aware do). A question that names a problem the reader recognises, in the words they would use, followed by a line that names the category or product. Example: a question about lying awake at 3, above a made bed, then one line naming the blanket. Ask about the situation, never about the viewer's body or condition.

**Outcome plus specifics.** Solution-aware. A plain sentence that says what the buyer gets, then two to four hard facts beside the product (grams, pockets, hours, layers). Example: a sentence about what a weekend bag holds, with four spec labels stacked down its side.

**Contrast in one line.** Problem-aware and solution-aware. Two states set against each other: from one state to another, a high number against a low number, or a false choice resolved with "both". Example: a percentage of energy the reader starts the day with now against the percentage they could, above an "on" switch graphic.

**First-person or quoted line as the headline.** Problem-aware and solution-aware; quoted testimonials sit in 13% of solution-aware images. A customer's short emotional sentence, set large, with the name its source gives. Word for word, or not used. Stars only if the brand's facts hold the rating.

**Reframe.** Unaware and stage 5 markets. Give a commodity a new role or stance: a baby blanket as a cape, a power bank as a reaction against dull colours. In the saved ads, 1 line over a strong photo. In this app an unaware ad's picture carries no headline: the reframe is the first sentence of the primary text.

**Category borrow.** Solution-aware. State that this gives the benefits of a known category in a new form or for a new occasion. Example: a lap-sized version of a weighted blanket described through the category the reader already knows.

**Offer as headline.** Most-aware (81% of 59 carry a discount). Event name, amount, and scope in that order. The largest type on the image is the number.

**Announcement.** Product-aware (25% of 64 use "new" or launch wording). "New", "back", a colour or edition name, then the brand.

### Hero visual slot

- **Product in hand or in use.** A hand holding, pouring, squeezing or wearing the product gives scale and realism.
- **Product on a colour-matched backdrop.** The background takes the product's own colour for a clean, catalogue look.
- **Texture close-up.** Fabric weave, metal shine, powder, liquid mid-pour. Common where touch sells the product.
- **Metaphor object.** A familiar object stands in for a body concern, which avoids showing skin or symptoms. Example: a fruit with half its peel textured and half smooth, used for a face cream.
- **Problem and fix in one frame.** The lost wallet on the floor below the phone alert that found it.
- **Open and packed.** Bags and organisers shown open and full, with each compartment labelled.
- **Styled result plus parts.** The finished look on a person above, the individual items in a row below.

### Support copy slot

- **Callout lines to the product.** Three to six short labels joined to the product by thin lines. Callout layouts cover 38% of problem-aware and 35% of solution-aware images.
- **Pill or badge chips.** A label each, floating around the product, each answering one practical doubt (washable, fits full bottles, carry-on size).
- **Benefit row.** Up to three benefits in a row at the base, each a phrase a person would say, often one felt benefit, one functional, one material.
- **Checklist.** Ticks for you, crosses for the alternative. Check or cross marks appear in 10% of problem-aware and 8% of solution-aware images.
- **Numbered steps or parts.** Step 1, 2, 3 for routines; 1 to 4 for items in a bundle.
- **Timeline.** Month-by-month milestones for products that take time, which also sets expectations.

### Proof slot

- **Review count with stars** near the headline or on a round badge (11% overall; 15% of problem-aware). Place it where the eye enters, usually top left or directly under the headline.
- **A single large statistic** with its timeframe. Use only figures the client can support, and say what was measured.
- **Review cards**, two or three staggered, set in the brand's own type (never a named review site's design). Each is a real customer's words with its source. Let a real customer make the comparison you would not make yourself.
- **Before and after data.** Two cards side by side with a clear "before" and "after" label. Only with measured figures the brand holds.
- **Authority cues**: certification seals on pack, "patented", press logos, a publication-style frame, and only the ones the brand has earned. Clinical or authority wording appears in 21% of solution-aware images.
- **Customer total.** "Join" plus a number, or "over" plus a number, as a short line.

### Offer slot

- **Round badge in a corner** for a first-order discount, kept small so the product stays the hero.
- **Footer ribbon** that holds offer and guarantee together, separate from the argument above.
- **Price reframed per use.** Pack price beside price per serving or per day.
- **Receipt comparison.** An itemised list of what the reader pays now, totalled, beside one product and its price. Every figure is the brand's, or a quoted customer's.
- **Value stack.** Each free item listed with its value and a total saved, as the brand's offer states them.
- **Struck-through price** next to the new price in a coloured block.
- **Threshold gift.** The free item named first, the spend condition second.
- **Entry price for premium goods.** "Starting under" a round figure over an opulent photo.

### Brand and call-to-action slots

- Brand name or logo sits top centre or bottom centre. It is the first readable line in 35% of images. In a generated ad the product carries the logo; it is not typed as a line of its own, and a native format carries none.
- A button-style call to action is transcribed on only 51 (9%); product-aware 16%, most-aware 15%. When used, it is two or three words and often specific to the ad's angle: a prompt to see the gift guide in a gifting ad, or to reserve one in a restock ad.
- If the image carries no call to action, make sure the ad's button and primary text do.

## Layout archetypes

What saved winners did. The skill's rules still hold on every one.

**Product hero with callouts.** Solution-aware and problem-aware; anything with parts, materials or ingredients worth pointing at (bags, bedding, tools, skincare). Headline top, product centre, three to six labelled lines around it, proof badge, logo. Adapt by choosing callouts that answer the category's known doubts.

**Split comparison.** Solution-aware (27%) and problem-aware (19%). Left is the old way or before, right is the product or after. Keep crop and lighting identical so only the difference shows. Objects, not bodies, unless the brand uploaded real, consented photos. A variation labels the sides "before" and "new" to launch an improved product.

**Us versus them table.** Solution-aware (12% of 261; near zero elsewhere). Rows of attributes, ticks and crosses, product photos at the top of each column. Give the alternative credit on the basic row and win on the rest. Compare with a category, never a named competitor.

**Testimonial led.** Any stage except most-aware. A real quote as headline, the name its source gives, product below. No generated face beside it.

**Native interface.** Problem-aware (15%) and product-aware (14%). A notes-app checklist, a comment thread that asks and answers the main doubt, a phone notification, a story with caption stickers, an incoming-call screen for an offer. Reads as personal, not as an ad. Draw your own generic interface; do not copy a platform's exact design.

**Editorial frame.** Problem-aware and solution-aware. An article-style headline and breadcrumb over a product photo, with one phrase highlighted. Must be labelled honestly and must not imitate a real publication.

**Lifestyle photo with one line.** Unaware (94% of 48 are lifestyle or editorial) and stage 5 markets. Full-bleed photo, the brand, at most 1 plain line. Works for fashion, home and jewellery; weak alone for products that need explaining. In this app an unaware ad's picture carries no headline, and the story is in the primary text.

**Offer tile.** Most-aware (93% of 59). Event, number, scope, product, urgency line. Variants: a 3 by 3 product grid with the offer in the centre tile, a 2 by 2 bundle breakdown with one tile for the offer, a flat lay framing the number.

**Bundle or set breakdown.** Solution-aware, product-aware and most-aware. Items numbered or joined by plus signs, a small inset of the set in use, one price.

**Statistic led.** Problem-aware and solution-aware. One or two large numbers with direction and timeframe, product to one side.

### What the eye lands on first

From the analyst's layout notes across the sample read for this file (about 100 images):

- In copy-led layouts, the headline is top or top left in the largest type, and the eye then drops to the product in the centre or lower half.
- In offer tiles, the discount number is the largest element and the product frames it.
- In lifestyle layouts, the face or the product texture is first; text sits in clear space or directly on the product surface.
- One accent colour is used to pull the eye to a single word, a badge or the price.
- Arrows, pours and hands are used as pointers from headline to product.
- 9:16 layouts usually stack headline in the top third and product in the lower two thirds, leaving the very top and bottom edges clear.

## Patterns by awareness stage

**Unaware (n=48).** The least on the picture of any stage. A photo does the work: 94% lifestyle or editorial, 62% visual-led hooks, numbers in only 33%. Many carried only a mood line or the brand, which the analyst marks as too thin for a product that needs explaining. 34 of 48 are identity or experience led. In this app the picture carries no headline and the primary text tells the story.

**Problem-aware (n=157).** The headline names the problem (43% pain callout, 22% question mark, 27% audience callout). Then a named fix, three or so support points, and a proof badge. Callouts (38%), checklists, native-interface frames and metaphor visuals are common. Guarantees appear here more than at any other stage (5%).

**Solution-aware (n=261).** The fullest images. The reader knows the category, so the image argues difference: comparison (27% split layouts, 12% us-versus-them), ingredients and doses, specs, clinical wording (21%), quoted reviews (13%), time-bound claims (8%). Benefit headlines are most common here (26%).

**Product-aware (n=64).** Announcements and reminders: new colour, new edition, restock, bundle (25% use launch wording; 31% grid or catalogue). Offers start to appear (25% offer hooks) alongside social proof and the most on-image call-to-action text (16%).

**Most-aware (n=59).** The offer is the ad: 81% carry a discount, 90% a number, 17% a price, 14% urgency wording, 0% quoted testimonials or questions. Layout is an offer tile over a hero product, grid or flat lay, with the event name and any end date.

## Do not transfer

The analyst wrote a caution for every ad. Recurring themes, with keyword-match counts (n=589):

- **Brand names, logos, taglines and product names** (467, 79%).
- **Visual identity**: palette, fonts, packaging, props, the exact layout of a distinctive ad (444, 75%).
- **Specific claims and numbers** without your own substantiation (293, 50%): review counts, ratings, percentages with decimals, "number one", "clinically", "patented", customer totals.
- **Exact wording** (198, 34%): headlines, puns, rhymes, coined phrases. Borrow the sentence shape, write new words.
- **Health and medical claims** (124, 21%): drug names, hormone pathways, symptoms and conditions, asterisked claims without a visible disclaimer.
- **Offer terms** (115, 20%): discount depth, code, credit value, gift, guarantee length. Use the client's real terms.
- **Copy too thin for a cold audience.** The analyst repeatedly warns that brand-only or mood-only images work for known brands and retargeting, not for products that need explaining.
- **Platform and third-party design** (26, 4%): exact notes-app, review-site, notification or social-post interfaces; press logos; publication mastheads.
- **Likeness and names** (18, 3%): customer names, handles, creators, models.
- **Invented urgency or popularity** (12, 2%): "viral", waitlists, sold out, back in stock. Only if true.
- Also flagged: typos carried on the image, low-contrast headline colours, named competitor comparisons, cultural references that only fit one audience.

## Before you finalise, compare against winners

1. Is the awareness stage named, and does the headline type match that stage's common pattern (problem or question for problem-aware, difference for solution-aware, news for product-aware, offer for most-aware)?
2. Are the added words 20 or fewer, and is every line a plain sentence or a phrase a person would say, clear alone to a stranger?
3. Is there one clear first landing point, and a path from it to the product?
4. Does every support line answer a doubt this buyer has, as a short plain sentence or a phrase a person says?
5. Is there one proof element that is real, specific and placed near the headline?
6. If an offer is shown, are amount, scope and condition all stated, in the client's real terms?
7. Is the product clearly visible and identifiable, with the brand readable on it (a native format excepted)?
8. Is the layout uncluttered at phone size, with safe space at the top and bottom of a 9:16 frame?
9. Does the ad have a call to action somewhere, on the image or in the button?
10. Have you removed every borrowed name, phrase, number, interface and claim, and can the brand support every claim that remains?
