# Scroll-Stopper Static Ads

An image model's default ad is a centred product, a floating headline and a realistic picture of the benefit. It is easy to scroll past. This skill forces the decisions that make 1 static hard to pass: a problem the buyer feels, a lever that stops the thumb, a metaphor that makes the problem visible, and a headline that names it.

It is judged on 2 things. The buyer feels the problem in half a second, before reading. And the ad could only be this brand's: the product is in it, drawn true, and nothing in the picture promises what the brand cannot support.

## In this app

- This skill is style guidance for the "surreal" look of a static ad cut from an approved script. The script has already decided the argument, the claim, the proof and the offer. The skill chooses how the picture and the headline make that argument felt. It never adds a claim.
- The planner is sent `CARD.md`. This file is the reasoning behind it, for a person.
- How the product is shown true (tagged reference photos, the words agreeing with the photo, a check after the render) is `skills/production-principles`. How the image prompt is written (type by attributes, zones, exact words once, safe areas per ratio) is `skills/image-prompt-craft`. Which model renders is `skills/production-principles/models.md`. None of it is repeated here.
- Words on the image are a choice in the app: on, off, or on with a copy without them. With words off, the picture carries the problem alone.

## Order of decisions (copy first)

The biggest mistake is starting with the image. The picture is decided last, because it serves the words and the lever.

1. **Problem:** what the buyer feels, in their words: the moment, the annoyance, the fear. Not what the product does.
2. **Angle:** how the ad attacks it: reframe, agitate, stakes, humour or proof. The angle sets the emotional register.
3. **Headline:** written now, from a formula below. It names the problem, never the product.
4. **Sub-headline:** 1 line that gives the mechanism or reason why, in the brand's own words.
5. **Lever:** which of the 5 levers makes this hard to pass. The angle usually points to one.
6. **Metaphor:** the scene that makes the problem felt in half a second.
7. **Style:** chosen for the register the angle needs.
8. **Frame:** where the words, the product and the quiet areas sit.

Skip 1 to 4 and jump to "what is a cool picture" and the result is a beautiful empty scene.

## The product in the picture

The problem leads; the product is still there. An ad with no product sells the category, and an ad with a product drawn from words shows something the buyer will not receive.

- The product is in every ad, small and true: on a surface inside the scene, or in the block that carries its name and the mechanism line. It comes from the product's own photos (`skills/production-principles`, section 1): same shape, same colours, same printing. Never redrawn from a description, never a loose cut-out floating in a corner.
- In a drawn or modelled style it is redrawn in that style and keeps its shape, colours and what is printed large. Small print is left out, never invented.
- The product may be shown working when that is the turn of the ad and it is something a person would see (`Visible result` in the brain): an egg sliding in the pan, rain beading on the sleeve.
- A product block names the product and gives the mechanism line. It carries an offer only if the brand runs one, in the brand's words.

## An honest picture

A metaphor makes a problem felt. It is not evidence, and it must not look like evidence the brand does not have.

- The picture shows the problem, its cause or how the product works. It never shows a result the brand's facts do not state: no changed face or body, no before and after, no invented timeline, curve or number.
- A split comparison is the usual way against this product on 1 stated difference, unbranded. It is not the same person before and after.
- No picture tells the reader they have a condition, and none draws a medical outcome. Stakes are losses the buyer has really had.
- A generated person is a fictional adult and is never shown as a customer, an expert or the founder. No children.

## The 5 scroll-stop levers

"Something is wrong in this picture" is only 1 way to stop a thumb. Pick the lever the angle needs.

| Lever | What it does | Use for | It passes when | Consumable, durable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Impossible** | A size or a physics that cannot be, played straight | Intrigue, humour, a dramatised cost | No camera could take it. Slightly exaggerated is not impossible | A tiny crew sweeping a mountain of half-used jars; tiny workers resurfacing a giant frying pan |
| **2. Recognition** | The problem visible on the person or thing it happens to | A problem the buyer lives with | The buyer thinks "that's me", not "that's interesting" | A face reflected in a mirror 1 size too small for it; a shirt wet only along the seams of a "waterproof" jacket |
| **3. Humour** | The buyer's own phrase drawn literally | Light problems, awkward subjects | Someone would screenshot it for a friend | A cupboard avalanche of protein tubs; a drawer that is a graveyard of dead pans |
| **4. Stakes** | A real loss at its real size: money, time, a ruined thing | A cost the buyer keeps paying | The loss is one the buyer has had. Never a medical outcome | A bin of half-eaten snack bars with their prices; a suitcase split open on a carousel |
| **5. Plain information** | Looks like a note, a chart or a diagram, not an ad | Sceptical buyers, mechanism and proof | It has no polish. A slick infographic reads as an ad | A marker sketch of what a cleanser strips out; a pencil cutaway of 3 layers of fabric |

The honest test for every lever: does the picture make the problem felt in half a second?

## The 14 metaphor types

| # | Type | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Problem worn | The problem made physical on the person or object that has it | Pet fur matted on a uniform; rain soaked through at the shoulder seams |
| 2 | Scale distortion | 1 thing impossibly large or small | Tiny workers on a giant pan |
| 3 | Inside view | A cross-section that makes a hidden cause or mechanism visible | Water passing a stitched seam and stopping at a taped one |
| 4 | Symbol swap | An everyday object stands in for the failing thing | A sieve worn as a rain hat |
| 5 | Problem inventory | 1 figure or object with its small problems labelled around it | A suitcase with "wobbly wheel", "stuck zip", "cracked corner" |
| 6 | Journey | The same character or object at stages, in order | A pan at day 1, month 6, year 5 |
| 7 | Chart as hero | A chart is the picture | Cost per year of a pan that is replaced against one that is not |
| 8 | Cinematic stakes | Dark, dramatic light carries the loss | A lone ruined dinner under 1 hard light |
| 9 | Split comparison | 1 frame, the usual way against this product, close up | Stitched seam, taped seam, both under rain |
| 10 | Drawn scene | Flat illustration, for humour or a private subject | 2 pairs of feet under a duvet |
| 11 | Sketch | Hand-drawn, for teaching and trust | A sock with arrows showing where it grips |
| 12 | Numbered walkthrough | 3 numbered notes on a sketch that make a 3-step argument | 1 the cause, 2 why the usual fix misses it, 3 what reaches it |
| 13 | Product as protagonist | The product as a character that goes after the problem | A jar in a cape chasing junk-food villains down a corridor |
| 14 | Heritage line | A material or method shown through time | Figures from several eras wearing the same fibre |

Types 6, 7, 9 and 14 show stages, figures or history. They show only the ones the brand's facts state: a made-up curve, timeline or result is a made-up claim.

## Copy first: the headline before the picture

2 rules govern all of it:

- **The headline names the problem, the moment or the wrong belief. Never the product or the benefit.** The product's name belongs in the product block.
- **The offer never goes in the headline.** It lives in the product block, and only as the brand states it.

### The 8 headline formulas

| Formula | Shape | Consumable, durable |
|---|---|---|
| **Exact-moment question** | Name the precise moment as a question | "Face tight before you're even dressed?" · "Wet shoulders 10 minutes into the ride?" |
| **Myth-buster** | "X doesn't cause this. Y does." | "It isn't the cold. It's your cleanser." · "Your pan isn't worn out. Its coating is." |
| **Causal reframe** | "X is behind your Y" | "Your 4pm crash started at breakfast." · "Your waterproof leaks at the seams." |
| **Callout** | Who it is for, then the news | "Home cooks: you can stop buying a pan every 2 years." |
| **Stat-shock** | 1 specific, uncomfortable number from the brand's facts | "Your underwear is 60 to 90% polyester." |
| **First-person plea** | First person, urgent | "I'm begging you to stop scrubbing your face dry." |
| **Identification story** | 2 or 3 numbers as a small story, then "sound familiar?" | "3 pans. 6 years. All in the bin. Sound familiar?" |
| **Binary contrast** | Loser and winner in 2 beats | "Coatings wear out. Steel wears in." |

An exact-moment question names a moment, never a condition the reader has: ad platforms reject copy that tells a person what is wrong with them, and so does this app.

### The reframe (the core engine)

The move that makes an ad feel like a discovery, not a pitch: take a problem the buyer blames on the wrong cause and point it at a cause the product deals with. "It isn't the cold. It's your cleanser." Reach for it whenever the research shows the buyer blaming the wrong thing. The new cause must be one the brand's facts support.

### Make it theirs

- **Specific beats vague.** Exact numbers, and only the brand's own: a price, a count, a guarantee, a figure with a source.
- **The buyer's own words.** The phrase from the research, not the category's phrase for it.
- **Nothing is misspelt or starred out to get past an ad platform's review.** If a word cannot run, the idea is rewritten.

## Choosing: angle to lever to metaphor to style to formula

| Angle | Lever | Metaphor type | Style | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agitate a felt problem | Recognition | Problem worn, problem inventory | Stylised 3D, or photographic with 1 exact detail | Exact-moment question |
| Reframe a wrong cause | Recognition or plain information | Inside view, split comparison | Clay, sketch | Myth-buster, causal reframe |
| Prove the mechanism | Plain information | Inside view, chart, sketch, numbered walkthrough | Sketch, chart | Causal reframe |
| Make them laugh | Humour | Scale distortion, symbol swap, product as protagonist | Stylised 3D, drawn | Binary contrast |
| Show the cost | Stakes | Cinematic stakes, journey | Dark photographic | Stat-shock, identification story |
| A private subject | Recognition, softened | Drawn scene | Flat illustration | Exact-moment question |
| A fact that shocks | Impossible | Scale distortion, symbol swap | Photographic composite | Stat-shock |
| A material with a past | Plain information | Heritage line | 3D figures | Callout |

**Style follows register.** Stylised 3D and clay read as approachable and funny; a photographic composite reads as urgent and real; flat illustration softens a private subject; a sketch reads as teaching; dark cinematic light carries a loss. Name no studio or film as a style.

**The photographic trap.** Photographic style lands when the scene holds 1 impossible or exact element. It fails when it is a realistic person merely feeling a feeling, because that is where a generated image shows. If the idea is "a person feeling something", draw them in stylised 3D or clay.

## The frame

- **The picture makes the claim before a word is read.** If the picture has to be explained, it is the wrong picture.
- **The problem takes most of the frame,** and is the first thing in the scene description. Models weight what comes first; an impossible element buried mid-scene is made ordinary.
- **Scale is said as a ratio,** against a hand or a person ("workers no taller than the pan's rivet"), with the ordinary-sized thing in the frame beside it. Without the anchor, impossible collapses to "a bit big".
- **The problem lives on someone or something.** A person, an animal or an object the buyer owns shows it. An abstract landscape has to be interpreted; a soaked shirt does not.
- **"Wrong" is specific and human, not abstract and beautiful.** Recognised beats interesting. If the picture is merely nice, it will not stop anyone.
- **Subjects crop at the edges.** A centred, padded composition reads as a template.
- **Words sit in a designed container** (a band, a pill, a box, a brush shape) in a zone the scene leaves quiet. Where the zones may sit in each shape is the app's rule.
- **2-tone headline.** The set-up in a neutral colour, the punchline in the accent. At scroll speed the eye reads only the accent words, so they must make sense alone.
- **2 colours.** 1 dominant tone and 1 high-contrast accent, the accent only on the punchline and the product block. A cinematic palette has no hierarchy.

## What the corpus shows

The 5 levers, the metaphor types and the formulas were read off 23 winning ads, torn down in `references/winning-ads-teardown.json` (per ad: problem, angle, metaphor, exact copy, design system). Almost all are health, pet and apparel products, and many make claims this app would not: read them for how the chain of decisions runs, never for words, claims or brands to copy.

- **1 designed artifact, not a scene with a caption.** The composition is designed around where the words sit.
- **Style follows register; there is no house style.** Roughly a third photographic composites, a third stylised 3D or clay, the rest illustration, sketch and data pictures.
- **Recognition converts; interesting does not.** Fur on a uniform, a foot swollen like a balloon: specific, a little embarrassing, plainly somebody's life.
- **The product block floats inside the scene, above the bottom band** of a tall frame, where the feed's own buttons sit.

## Known failure modes

1. **The wrong element is too tasteful.** Push from "clever metaphor" to "this is specifically me".
2. **Small labels misrender.** 3 words at most to a label. When one fails, shorten it; do not make the whole ad again unchanged.
3. **The product does not match its photo.** Say again, in the same words as before, the 2 or 3 things that make it this product, and attach the photo with its job.
4. **A realistic person looks generated.** Change the style, not the prompt.
