# Scroll-stopper statics: rules

1 picture that makes the buyer's problem felt in half a second, before a word is read, with a headline that names the problem. Judged on that, and on whether the ad could only be this brand's. It changes how the argument is shown, never what is claimed.

## Order of decisions

Problem in the buyer's words, then the angle (reframe, agitate, stakes, humour, proof), then the headline, then the sub-headline, then the lever, then the metaphor, then the style. The picture is chosen last, to serve the words.

## The 5 levers: pick 1

- **Impossible:** a size or a physics that cannot be, played straight. Passes when no camera could take it. (Tiny workers resurfacing a giant frying pan.)
- **Recognition:** the problem visible on the person or thing it happens to. Passes when the buyer thinks "that's me". (A shirt wet only along the seams of a "waterproof" jacket.)
- **Humour:** the buyer's own phrase drawn literally. Passes when someone would send it to a friend. (A drawer that is a graveyard of dead pans.)
- **Stakes:** a real loss at its real size: money, time, a ruined thing. Passes when the loss is one the buyer has had. Never a medical outcome.
- **Plain information:** looks like a note or a diagram, not an ad. Passes when it has no polish. Shows only what the brand's facts state.

## The 14 metaphor types

1. Problem worn: the problem made physical on the person or object that has it.
2. Scale distortion: 1 thing impossibly large or small, said as a ratio against a hand or a person.
3. Inside view: a cross-section that shows the hidden cause or mechanism.
4. Symbol swap: an everyday object stands in for the failing thing (a sieve for a leaking jacket).
5. Problem inventory: 1 figure or object with its small problems labelled around it, 3 words each.
6. Journey: the same character or object at stages, in order.
7. Chart as hero: a chart is the picture.
8. Cinematic stakes: dark, dramatic light on the loss.
9. Split comparison: 1 frame, the usual way against this product, close up.
10. Drawn scene: flat illustration, for humour or a private subject.
11. Sketch: hand-drawn, for teaching and trust.
12. Numbered walkthrough: 3 numbered notes on a sketch that make a 3-step argument.
13. Product as protagonist: the product as a character that goes after the problem.
14. Heritage line: a material or method shown through time.

Types 6, 7, 9 and 14 show stages, figures or history: only the ones the brand's facts state.

## The 8 headline formulas

1. Exact-moment question: "Wet shoulders 10 minutes into the ride?" Name the moment, never a condition the reader has.
2. Myth-buster: "Your pan isn't worn out. Its coating is."
3. Causal reframe: "Your waterproof leaks at the seams."
4. Callout: who it is for, then the news. No false alarm.
5. Stat-shock: 1 uncomfortable number, from the brand's facts.
6. First-person plea: "I'm begging you to stop scrubbing your face dry."
7. Identification story: "3 pans. 6 years. All in the bin. Sound familiar?"
8. Binary contrast: "Coatings wear out. Steel wears in."

The headline names the problem, not the product or the offer. The sub-headline is the mechanism in 1 line, in the brand's own words. Numbers and the buyer's phrases come from the brand's facts and research.

## Choosing

| Angle | Lever | Metaphor | Style | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agitate a felt problem | Recognition | 1, 5 | stylised 3D, or photographic with 1 exact detail | 1 |
| Reframe a wrong cause | Recognition or information | 3, 9 | clay, sketch | 2, 3 |
| Prove the mechanism | Plain information | 3, 7, 11, 12 | sketch, chart | 3 |
| Make them laugh | Humour | 2, 4, 13 | stylised 3D, drawn | 8 |
| Show the cost | Stakes | 8, 6 | dark photographic | 5, 7 |
| A private subject | Recognition, softened | 10 | flat illustration | 1 |
| A fact that shocks | Impossible | 2, 4 | photographic composite | 5 |
| A material with a past | Plain information | 14 | 3D figures | 4 |

- A realistic person merely feeling something looks generated: draw them in stylised 3D or clay. Photographic style is for a scene with 1 impossible or exact element. Name no studio.
- "Wrong" is specific and human, not abstract and beautiful. A recognised moment beats an interesting image.

## The frame

- The problem takes most of the frame and is the first thing in the scene description. Subjects crop at the edges.
- The product follows the choice made for this ad. For an unaware or problem aware buyer the default is no product: the problem is the whole picture, and no pack, name or logo is in it. Where the product is asked for, it is small and true: on a surface in the scene, or in the block that holds its name and the mechanism line. From its photo, the same shape and colours, never redrawn from words and never a loose cut-out.
- Words sit in a designed container (a band, a pill, a box) in a zone the scene leaves quiet. 2-tone headline: the set-up neutral, the punchline in the accent colour; the accent words alone still make sense.
- 2 colours: 1 dominant tone and 1 accent.
- An offer goes in the product block, never the headline, and only as the brand states it. An ad with no product carries no offer.
- If the ad has no words on the image, the picture carries the problem alone.

## An honest picture

- It shows the problem, its cause or how the product works. Never a result the brand's facts do not state: no changed face or body, no before and after, no invented timeline or curve.
- It never tells the reader they have a condition, and never draws a medical outcome.
- A word is never misspelt or starred out to get past an ad platform's review.
