# Format: CGI explainer

Where this and the task skill differ, this wins.

An explainer is judged on 2 things: a stranger could retell the mechanism to a friend in 1 sentence, and every line and every picture is something the brand can support.

## What makes it an explainer

Rendered 3D shows what no camera can: the inside of a body, a fibre at 1,000 times its size, 100 years in 3 seconds. **Scale is the device:** something very large or very small, seen as if you were standing inside it. Nothing is filmed or handheld. People, where there are any, are stylised rendered adults, clearly not photographs, fully clothed, seen whole or from behind. The product is the 1 real thing: its real shape and printing, exactly.

## The mechanism: 1, and only what the brand can support

Use this format only when all 4 are true. If 1 fails, say so in the notes and show the mechanism as plainly as the facts allow; do not stretch a claim to fill the format.

- A camera can't show it. (If the result can be watched, a demonstration is the better ad.)
- It is 1 thing acting on another: "A meets B, B does C, so you get D."
- Every link is in the brand's facts, or is common knowledge a school book states.
- A regular buyer of the category would not already know it.

The same test holds in any category. A mineral drink: what the mineral does as the body winds down, as far as the claim goes. A pan: oil baking into a film on hot steel. A wool top: a fibre taking up vapour before it turns to sweat on the skin.

Keeping it honest:

- Claims are worded "supports" or "helps", never "treats", "cures", "prevents" or "fixes".
- The picture is a claim too. It shows the product doing as much as the line's verb and no more: for "supports", the product taken or used and the process carrying on. No damage vanishing, no before and after of a body, no organ or structure the facts do not name.
- A number is used only if the brand's facts hold it with a source.
- **1 technical noun:** the ingredient, material or part the mechanism turns on. It has 1 shape and 1 colour wherever it is shown. No second technical term.

## What it is good for

It holds attention by curiosity and has no believed human face. So it carries the **mechanism**. It is weak for proof from people and for offers.

By stage: solution-aware buyers first (why this works when the others did not), and problem-aware buyers when the cause is not the one they assume. For an unaware buyer it opens on the body, the home or the kit, never on the brand.

## What to avoid

- A second mechanism, or a list of ingredients or features.
- An ending on a feeling ("feel like yourself again"). End on what the buyer does: carries the shopping up the stairs, flips the egg.
- Fear: illness, decay, a clinic.
- Any phrase, name or manner that belongs to a known narrator or channel.

## What this reads from the brand brain

- `products-and-positioning.md`, the product: `How it works` (proposed: the mechanism as 2 or 3 links with their sources, and the usual way and why it falls short), `Key features`, `Core benefit`, `How it is used`, `Visible result`.
- `brand.json`: claims with sources (every link and every number), forbidden claims, offers.
- `customer-avatars.md`, the avatar: `What almost stops them` and the `Customer language bank` (the assumption that is turned over), `The moment` (what the buyer does again).
- `competitors.md`: `Gaps worth owning` (what gets in the way, never by name). `brain.md`: `Market sophistication`.
- `brand-guidelines.md`: `Brand guardrails`.

`How it works` blank: build the chain only from claims that have a source, and mark it "inferred" in the notes for the brand to confirm. A link with no source is left out, not written.
