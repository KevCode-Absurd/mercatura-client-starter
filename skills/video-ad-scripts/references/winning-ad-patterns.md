# Winning video ad patterns

## What this is and how to use it

This file summarises patterns found in 486 analysed video ads from a curated library of e-commerce ads that were saved as winners. Use it to see how a beat is made visible, to check your pacing and text against what is common, and to find a proof or opening device that fits the brief.

How to use it:

1. The awareness stage comes with the request. Most patterns below change by stage.
2. The script type and the controls sent with the request decide how the script opens and the order of its beats. Inside that, take at most 1 opening device, 1 proof device and 1 close from "Patterns by script beat". Do not stack every device into one script.
3. Compare your draft with "The numbers" and the checklist at the end.
4. Borrow a device only. Never reuse another brand's wording, claims, numbers, names or offer terms.

Honest limits:

- The library is curated. Someone judged these ads to be winners. No spend, ROAS, CTR or hold-rate data is attached, so nothing here is a test result.
- Counts show what is common among saved winners. They do not show what causes performance. A common pattern can simply be a common habit.
- Every field (awareness stage, timeline, transcript, tags) was written by a vision model, not a human analyst. Stage labels are judgement calls: 385 of 486 are marked high confidence, 101 medium.
- Several numbers come from keyword matching on that model's text. They are marked "heuristic". Treat them as rough.
- The mix leans to fashion, beauty, wellness, supplements, food and drink, jewellery and home textiles. 471 of 486 are in English. 325 distinct advertisers are named; the largest single advertiser has 14 ads.
- Timeline segments are the analyst's scene splits, so "seconds per segment" is a proxy for visual change, not a cut count.

### The first three seconds

- On-screen text is present in the first segment in 446 (92%) and within 3 seconds in 452 (93%). Only 23 (5%) have no on-screen text at all.
- The first on-screen text has a median of 9 words (n=446; middle half 5 to 13).
- The first segment lasts a median 2.5 s (middle half 1.9 to 3.7 s).
- The product or its packaging is described in the first segment's visual in 330 (68%), and within 3 seconds in 364 (75%). Heuristic: product-type nouns in the analyst's visual description. Most-aware ads: 18 of 18.

- The first on-screen text contains a question mark in 42 (9%), the word "POV" in 22 (5%), a digit in 111 (23%). For most-aware ads a digit appears in 16 of 18.

## Patterns by script beat

Each pattern lists when to use it and how to adapt it. Examples are described in our own words. The script type and the controls sent with the request decide the opening and the beat order; these patterns are for how a beat is shown. A device that needs something the brand has not supplied (a real customer, an expert, a real comment, a third party's screen) is not used.

### Opening, 0 to 3 seconds

**Text hook over a moving picture from frame one.** Near universal (92% have text in the first segment). Use at every stage. Write 5 to 13 words that work with sound off, and put motion under it, not a still. Example: a creator pulls a garment over her head while a two-word label sits on screen.

**Audience or identity callout.** Strongest in problem-aware ads (37% of 129). Name who it is for by age, habit or self-label, then give them one reason to stay. Adapt by using the words that audience uses for itself. Example: a woman walking to her car names an age group and a training habit before saying anything about the product.

**Problem re-enactment.** Problem-aware and solution-aware; best for products that fix a visible failure. Show the bad moment happening in the first two seconds, then answer it. Example: someone sits on a pair of sunglasses and snaps them; the next shot bends the advertised pair flat.

**Symptom question or "why does this keep happening" question.** Problem-aware (question marks in 19 of 129 first texts). Ask about a specific, observable symptom, then reveal a cause the viewer has not considered. Example: a presenter asks why hair that grows every month never gets longer, then answers that it breaks as fast as it grows.

**Objection as the hook (comment reply).** Product-aware and solution-aware; used for price and safety doubts (comment-reply device in 13 ads). Put the real doubt on screen as a comment sticker and answer it with a demonstration. Example: a comment asks why a blanket costs so much; the creator holds a washed one and a new one and asks the viewer to tell them apart. Only use real comments.

**Hands and texture first.** Unaware and solution-aware; tactile goods (textiles, jewellery, leather, beauty, drinks). Open on hands touching, pouring, opening or wearing the product. Works without speech. Example: a close shot of fingers running across a ribbed throw while the creator says she wishes the viewer could feel it.

**Category flaw reveal.** Solution-aware, stage 4 markets with many similar products. Open by saying the common choice has a hidden cost, then show it. Keep the claim checkable. Example: a creator reads the sugar figure off a competitor-style packet, then turns the advertised packet to show its own panel.

**List or negative list.** Problem-aware. A numbered promise holds attention through the count; the product is one item, usually the last. Example: three ways to wind down in the evening, where the third is a bath with the product.

**Mid-action open.** Problem-aware story ads. Start inside the demanding moment, then cut back to the preparation. Example: an instructor is shown mid-class, then at her kitchen counter explaining what she takes before teaching.

**Offer first.** Most-aware only (78% of 18 lead with the offer). Put the discount, event name and brand on screen at second zero and keep them there.

### Dramatising the problem

- **Show the old way failing.** Spills, breakage, clutter, the stained version beside the clean one. Split screens appear in 41 ads (8%).
- **Tally the cost of trying.** A creator states what she spent or how many alternatives she cycled through, then names the simple fix.
- **Pick a micro-moment, not a general complaint.** The second coffee, the airline bag fee, taking earrings out every night. Specific moments let viewers self-select.
- **Colour shift.** Black-and-white for the before state, full colour once the product arrives.
- **Reframe the cause.** Tell the viewer the failure was not their fault and name a cause that the product addresses. Keep the cause true and supportable.

### Showing the mechanism

A mechanism or explanation beat appears in 197 (41%), usually around a third of the way in.

- **Physical analogy.** A dry sponge that cracks versus a damp one that bends, used to explain brittle hair.
- **Macro on the part that differs.** The flat back of an earring, the stitching on a boot, the weave of a fabric.
- **Visible evidence the product worked.** A clear chamber full of trimmed ends, residue on a cloth, a liquid changing colour as it mixes.
- **Read it off the pack.** Hold the nutrition or spec panel to the camera and compare one or two numbers.
- **Steps in order.** Two or three numbered steps with one overlay each, or a timed-release story told as fall asleep, stay asleep, wake up.
- **A stand-in for the body.** A silicone ear for piercings, a wig for a detangling brush. Useful where real footage would be unpleasant or restricted.
- **Short 3D render.** Often used only as the closing product shot with the key specs.

### Proof

A proof beat appears in 290 (60%), first showing around a fifth of the way in. Do not hold proof for the end.

- **Stress test or worst case.** Run water over the boot, tangle the wig on purpose, bend the frames.
- **Uncut take.** One continuous overhead shot of everything going into the bag removes doubt about editing.
- **Review cards or comment bubbles stacking on screen** over product footage (25 ads use review or screenshot overlays).
- **A third-party artefact.** A sleep-tracker screen, a retailer shelf, a podcast clip beside product footage. Use only with rights and only if real.
- **Numbers on screen.** Review counts, units sold, years as a customer. Review counts or star ratings appear in 46 (9%).
- **Fit facts for apparel.** The presenter's height and size printed on screen during a try-on.
- **Reaction of the recipient** for gifts, followed by a shot of the item being made.
- **Expert or professional presenter** in 33 (7%). A nurse, a piercer, a maker in the workshop. Only a real one, from the brand's own footage: never a generated person given a title.

### Offer and call to action

An offer or call-to-action beat appears in 278 (57%), starting at a median 77% of runtime. A call-to-action or end-card cue sits in the last segment in 287 (59%).

- **End card.** Logo, product, one line, a button-style prompt or URL. Two to four seconds.
- **Offer plus risk reversal stacked together** at the close: discount, guarantee, free shipping or returns, review badge.
- **Reason for the price.** Seasonal event, first order, bundle, restock, retail launch date. A stated reason reads better than a bare percentage.
- **Deadline tied to a real date.** Order-by dates for gifting, sale end dates.
- **Spoken prompt plus pointing gesture** in creator ads, matched by a one-line overlay.
- **Colour or variant spread** just before the close, so the viewer has a choice to make.
- **Pinned banner.** For sale ads, keep the offer on screen the whole time (13 of 18 most-aware ads pin their text).

### Pacing and visual change

- Median 3.7 seconds per segment overall; 2.5 in unaware ads, 4.0 in problem-aware ads (n=486).
- No-speech ads run about 2.6 seconds per segment and 13 seconds total. Spoken ads run about 4.4 seconds per segment and 33 seconds total.
- Plan about 7 distinct visual beats for a 20 to 30 second script. Give every spoken sentence its own picture.
- Loops and variant swaps (same frame, product changes every 1 to 2 seconds) are the standard pattern for sale and catalogue ads under 12 seconds.

### Text overlay conventions

- Short lines that restate or compress the speech, so the ad works muted. The analyst notes caption-style text matching speech in 80 of 263 spoken ads (heuristic, likely an undercount).
- One idea per overlay. Change the overlay when the picture changes.
- Label things: product name, step number, shade name, item in a gift guide.
- First-person overlays written like a caption, for no-speech creator-style ads.
- Fixed header and footer with a rotating middle for catalogue and bundle ads.
- Emoji appear as light punctuation in creator-style overlays, rarely in brand-led ones.

## Patterns by awareness stage

**Unaware (n=65).** Short (median 15 s), mostly without speech (77%), fastest visual change. Visual hooks dominate (69%). 56 of 65 are identity or experience led, in fashion, jewellery, home and drinks. Structure: striking picture, product in use across settings, light or absent close. Only 37% end on a call-to-action cue and the analyst often flags that as a weakness. For a performance brief, keep the visual open and add a one-line label and a real close.

**Problem-aware (n=129).** Longest (median 28 s), most spoken (68%), most creator-led (59% UGC). Callout or pain hooks, then problem, cause, mechanism, demonstration, proof, offer. Problem-solution is the tagged approach in 66 of 129. Guarantees and free-shipping lines show up here more than elsewhere. 71% end on a call-to-action cue.

**Solution-aware (n=231).** The largest group and the most varied. Median 24 s. The viewer knows the category, so the script argues why this one: comparison, ingredient or feature breakdown, stress test, category flaw. Social proof hooks rise (12%). Both spoken creator ads and silent demo-plus-overlay ads are common (40% have no speech).

**Product-aware (n=43).** Median 18 s. Answers a specific doubt (price, fit, safety, whether it is worth it) or announces something new (shade, collection, retailer). Social proof hooks peak here (21%); urgency wording appears in 10 of 43. Comment-reply and washed-versus-new style demonstrations fit this stage.

**Most-aware (n=18, small sample).** Median 10 s, 89% without speech, half are motion graphics. Offer in the first frame, pinned throughout, product variants cycling underneath, event name and date. No problem or mechanism beats.

## Do not transfer

The analyst wrote a caution for every ad. Recurring themes, with keyword-match counts (n=486):

- **Brand names, logos, taglines and product names** (399, 82%). Never carry these over.
- **Visual identity**: palette, fonts, packaging, props, locations (300, 62%).
- **Specific claims and numbers** without your own substantiation (211, 43%): review counts, percentages, "x times more", clinical language, "number one".
- **Offer terms** (115, 24%): the discount depth, the code, the free-plus-shipping mechanic, the guarantee length. Use the client's real offer.
- **Health and medical claims** (65, 13%): hormones, sleep stages, weight, anything diagnostic. Check against platform policy and regulation.
- **Missing call to action** (61, 13%) and missing branding (41, 8%). These are flaws in saved ads, not features. Do not copy an unbranded, no-close ending into a conversion ad.
- **Music and voice** (50, 10%): trending tracks and synthetic voices are not licensed for you.
- **Likeness** (33, 7%): celebrities, podcast hosts, named customers, creator handles. A generated presenter is a fictional adult: no children, and no testimony of a personal result.
- **Review cards, comment stickers and third-party screens** are used only when the brand holds the real one, word for word. None is mocked up.
- **Invented urgency or popularity** (13, 3%): closing-down stories, "viral", sold-out comments, waitlists. Only if true.
- **Exact wording** (66, 14%): hook lines, puns, coined product verbs.
- Also flagged: competitor products visible in shot, typos in overlays, platform-specific trend framing that will not travel, and unsafe use shown on screen.

## Before you finalise, compare against winners

1. Does the opening match what the request's awareness stage most often uses ("Patterns by awareness stage"), inside the script type that was asked for?
2. Is there on-screen text in the first segment, around 5 to 13 words, readable with sound off?
3. Is something moving in the first 2.5 seconds, and is the product or its effect on screen within 3 seconds (seen, not yet named) unless the script type calls for a delayed reveal?
4. Is the length the one the request asks for? For comparison, winners run about 10 to 18 s with no speech, about 25 to 45 s spoken, about 10 s for an offer ad.
5. Does the spoken word count match the request's word target? Winners speak at about 3 to 3.5 words a second; a target below that leaves room for delivery.
6. Is there a new picture about every 3 to 4 seconds, and one for each spoken sentence?
7. Does proof arrive in the first third, shown and not only stated?
8. If the stage is problem-aware or solution-aware, is the mechanism made visible with a prop, close-up, residue or side-by-side?
9. Does the last segment carry brand, product and a clear next step, with offer and risk reversal stated in the client's real terms?
10. Have you removed every borrowed name, number, claim, phrase, track and likeness, and can the client support every claim that remains?
