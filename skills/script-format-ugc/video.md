# UGC as a video: 1 person talking to their phone

## The 5 beats

One shape that works, not a fixed order and not the only shape: the writer is given no order. The lengths follow the idea: each beat gets the room it needs.

1. **Hook, the ad's first moments.** Native, pile B: a thought, a question or a confession, mid-action, no greeting, no brand name. "I ordered this in 3 sizes because I had no idea which one I was" is the register. It is written with line 2 and judged with it.
2. **Setup.** Who this is for and the doubt they already have: the accommodation, said as something a person did or noticed. Name the doubt out loud ("I assumed it was another one of those"). About a quarter of the ad, and 1 sentence in a very short one.
3. **Demonstration.** The product in use, in hand, in the order it is really used. Usually the longest beat. Specifics over adjectives: what it looks like, what it does, how long it takes. The doubt from the setup is answered here by what is shown. If the mechanism needs saying, 1 or 2 plain sentences a person would repeat to a friend.
4. **Turn.** The honest result, and it claims less than it could. This is where the script is believed or not, and where most UGC fails.
5. **One close that works.** Soft. It still names the action and what happens next ("it's on their site, the starter size is the one to get"), said the way a person passes something on to a friend. No shouting, no "buy now", no urgency the offer does not have. Then, if it fits, 1 identity line.

## Rules for the words

- **The product's arrival** is the way people really mention products: a thought, a small complaint, a bit of story, then the thing. Seen in hand before it is named.
- **Person and tense:** 1st person past for a story, 2nd person present for a direct script.
- 1 moment of doubt or surprise.
- A script is not squeezed to a shorter length: a shorter version is its own script, written only if the hook and the turn still land without the build.
- **Hook variants:** each a different way in (a recognition, a question, the objection said first, a number, the demonstration itself), not 1 hook reworded. Each is read with line 2.

## Length

It follows the idea, never a cap. 15 to 60 seconds for most. A doubt talked through, or a story, needs 45 seconds or more: under about 30 there is no room for both the build and a bridge to the product. A routine or a discovery lands in 20 to 30.

## Visual track

- Selfie framing, real rooms, real light. The product comes into view when the words reach it, already in the hand.
- Demonstration is the strongest proof this format has: write the product being handled or used, 1 action per visual note, in the words the brand uses for how it opens and is used.
- Something changes on screen all through the ad, and it is a cut: closer on the key phrase, the hands from above, a second place for the "before", the product very close. A talking head alone loses people.
- Editorial text supports the line; subtitles transcribe approved speech only when enabled.

## A real creator

- Leave square-bracket placeholders where their own experience goes ("[what you tried before]", "[the week you noticed]"). Their true detail beats any written one. The 1 number and the turn are theirs.
- Write for how they talk. A creator who is obviously reading lines breaks the ad.

## An AI-generated presenter

The 5 beats hold, with these changes. Hook: a recognition, a question or the objection, not a confession. Setup: the doubt as the viewer's ("you've probably assumed..."). The number: a fact of the product or a count of customers, never the presenter's own week 3. Turn: what customers say, in their own words given as a quote (the source stays in the line's evidence), or what the product plainly does and does not do ("It won't change your life. It's 1 less thing to think about."), only as far as the brand's facts go.
