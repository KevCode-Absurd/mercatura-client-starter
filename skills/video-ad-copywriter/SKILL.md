# Video ad copywriter

Write video ad scripts that sell, working from what a brand already knows: the product, the offer, the brand voice, the ideal customer (ICP) and sometimes a reference ad. No reviews, tickets or call recordings are needed.

Without customer research, every script is a **hypothesis about the customer**. That shapes the whole method. You model the customer carefully, write in their likely words, mark what you assumed, and give the brand several distinct angles to test, because nobody can reliably pick the winning ad in advance. The market decides.

**Superseded (2026-10-05).** This is the original skill. Creative Studio does not send it to any step: scripts are written with `script-from-research` and its family, which say what to do when the brand brain is blank (write on what is there, and list what was assumed). It is kept for use outside the app, for a brand with no research at all. Its examples are a product bought once and kept (a pan) beside one that is used up and reordered (a coffee concentrate): the doubts, the proof and the call to action differ between the 2, and step 4 says how.

## Pick the mode

Match effort to the ask. A request for 5 hooks does not need the full workflow.

| Request | Mode | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "Give me hooks", "1 more headline line", "punch up this line" | Quick | Steps 1 and 6 only, then the hook rules. Return the lines. |
| "Write a script" | Full | All steps. 1 full script plus hook variants. |
| "Write scripts to test" or no angle given | Angle test | All steps. 3 scripts on 3 different angles. |
| "Make something like this ad" | Reference remix | Step 2 first, then the full workflow. |
| "What's wrong with this script?" | Review | Label each line's beat, critique beat by beat, then rewrite. |
| "Make it 15s" or "make a 3 minute version" | Length change | Re-plan the beats for the new length and rewrite. Never just trim. |

## Inputs

Use whatever the user gives. Ask only if the product itself is unclear. Otherwise make sensible assumptions and list them at the top.

| Input | Used for |
|---|---|
| Product: what it is, how it works, materials or ingredients, how it's used | Mechanism, demo, visuals |
| Price, offer, bundle, guarantee, shipping terms | Offer, risk reversal, CTA |
| Claims the brand can make, and any it can't | Compliance |
| Proof the brand has (reviews count, press, awards, certifications, a credentialed founder) | Proof beat |
| ICP: who buys, their situation, what they want | Customer model |
| Brand voice and words to use or avoid | Voice pass |
| Reference ad(s) | Structure to borrow |
| Angle, issue or offer the user wants to push | Concept |
| Format, length, platform, who delivers it | Script controls |

**Facts must come from the user.** Do not make up reviews, star ratings, customer counts, study results, percentages, awards, press mentions, doctor endorsements or customer stories. They are the first thing a viewer, a platform reviewer or a regulator checks, and a single invented number can sink a brand. When a beat needs proof you don't have, write a placeholder such as `[REVIEW_COUNT]` or `[CUSTOMER_QUOTE]` and list it at the end.

This includes small details that make UGC feel real: how much they paid for something else, how long they've had the problem, "since March", "20 minutes in". They read as facts on camera. Keep them general ("for months"), or put them in brackets for the creator to swap in their own true detail: `[HOW LONG YOU'VE HAD THE DESK]`.

Comparisons need support too. "Won't go flat like a yoga mat" or "cheap mats curl" claims something about other products. Use it only if the brief supports it, otherwise flag it.

The line to draw: 2nd person observations about the viewer's likely life are the whole point of an accommodation ("You stand on 1 leg by mid-afternoon"). Stated facts about a real person, the product or "most people" are claims, and claims need the brief behind them.

## Workflow

### Step 1: build the customer model

This replaces customer research. Think like the ICP for a few minutes before writing anything. Write the model down as hypotheses, short and concrete:

| Hypothesis | Question to answer | Example (non-stick pan buyer: bought once) | Example (cold-brew concentrate buyer: bought again) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Desire | What do they want that they already wanted before any ad? | To cook like they know what they're doing | A good coffee without leaving the house |
| Problem in their words | How would they say it to a friend? | "My pans are ruined again" | "The coffee I make at home is sludge" |
| Accommodation | What have they quietly changed in their life to cope? | Stopped searing steak. Cooks everything on medium. | Buys one on the way in every day and calls it a treat |
| Failed solutions | What did they try first, and why did it let them down? | A cheap non-stick set that peeled | A pod machine: burnt taste, a drawer of pods |
| Trigger moment | What day or event makes them look for a fix? | Flakes of coating in the scrambled eggs | Pouring the third cup of the week down the sink |
| Objections | Why wouldn't they buy? | Too heavy, too much fuss to care for, too expensive | It'll taste stale, I won't keep it up, a subscription I can't get out of |
| Their words | Nouns and phrases they use, never package language | "sticks", "peeling", "my pans" | "sludge", "bitter", "my morning coffee" |

The **accommodation** matters most. People rarely say their workaround out loud. Naming it makes a viewer feel seen in a way a symptom never does. "You've stopped searing steak at home" lands harder than "Non-stick pans wear out."

Good ways to find an accommodation: ask what they avoid, what they've stopped doing, what they do in secret, what they over-buy, what they plan their day around.

Mark the model as `Assumed customer model` in the output, so the brand knows these are bets to test and can swap in real customer language later.

### Step 2: deconstruct the reference ad (if given)

Borrow the structure, not the words. A competitor's long-running ad works for that brand, its audience and its proof. Copying its lines gives you none of that and invites legal trouble.

Break the reference down:

1. Line by line, which beat is each line doing? (Beats are in step 5.)
2. Hook type, format, length, pacing, who's on camera.
3. Where does the product first appear? How does the script bridge to it?
4. What is the 1 thing doing most of the persuading (the load-bearing beat)?
5. What makes it feel native rather than like an ad?

Then write your script on the same skeleton with your product, your customer model and new lines. Keep every beat the reference relies on, especially its reframe of the real cause, which is often what made it work. A reframe doesn't need the "it's not X, it's Y" construction: "The fight was never about her teeth. It was 2 minutes with no end in sight." Don't closely paraphrase the reference's distinctive lines either (swapping a word or 2 still reads as a copy), and share no phrase of 6 or more words with it. Return the breakdown as a short table before the script.

### Step 3: place the buyer

Pick 1 stage of awareness and state it. It decides the opening.

| Stage | They know | Open with |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Nothing about the problem | A curiosity gap or surprising fact that reveals the problem |
| Problem aware | The problem, not the fixes | The accommodation or the trigger moment |
| Solution aware | Fixes exist | Why the usual fixes fail |
| Product aware | Your product exists | Proof, objection handling, the offer |
| Most aware | Nearly ready, usually stuck on price | Offer and a real reason for now |

Cold traffic is usually problem or solution aware. Colder stages need more education and more length.

Also judge how tired the market is of claims. In crowded categories, plain claims ("the best pan") don't land. Lead with a **mechanism** (why this works differently) and close on **identity** (who the buyer becomes).

### Step 4: shape the offer and the angles

**Offer.** Check what the brand actually has: the thing, the price, the guarantee, a reason to buy now, and the effort involved. A script cannot promise a refund the offer doesn't have.

Frame the price with 1 of these:

- **Against the alternative:** "You'll buy 3 non-stick pans in the time this lasts."
- **Per day or per use:** "About 30 cents a meal for the next 10 years."
- **Against the cost of the problem:** "Less than what you've spent on pans that peeled."
- **Make the effort the price:** "Rinse, dry, wipe with oil. That's the whole routine."

For a product that is used up and reordered the same 4 frames read differently: against the alternative ("2 cafe coffees cost more than a week of this"), per day ("[PRICE_PER_CUP] a cup", only from the brand's own numbers), against the cost of the problem ("less than what the pod machine cost you"), the effort ("pour, add milk, done").

A guarantee sells when it is specific, a little uncomfortable for the seller, and matched to how long the buyer needs to find out. "Cook with it for 100 days. If it's not your favourite pan, send it back used." beats "30-day money back."

**How it is bought changes the close.** Bought once: the doubts are the price, the size or fit, whether it lasts, whether it can go back; answer with the thing put through something, the price against what it replaces, and the returns; the call to action helps them choose the right one. Bought again: the doubts are whether it works for them, whether they'll keep it up, whether they're locked in; answer with the first use shown and the real pause or cancel steps; the call to action says what the first order is and what it costs after.

**Angles.** A concept is persona × angle × offer. When the user hasn't fixed an angle, write 3 that differ in the argument, not just the wording. Example for the pan: (1) the accommodation angle, (2) the cost-over-time angle, (3) the "chefs don't use non-stick" contrarian angle.

**Specificity.** Swap every adjective for a fact the user gave you: a number, a material, a place, a time, a named process. "Pre-seasoned with 3 coats of flaxseed oil" beats "premium seasoning." Cut any claim the competitor's product could make just as well ("high quality", "free shipping", "family owned") unless the brief shows buyers care about it.

### Step 5: write the beats

Every script that sells does these 9 jobs, in roughly this order. Short scripts drop some; long ones repeat them.

| # | Beat | Job | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook | Earn the next line and pre-qualify the right viewer | Write it last (step 6) |
| 2 | Problem | Make them feel the consequence | Name the accommodation, not the symptom. Speak to 1 person. Don't solve it yet. |
| 3 | Promise | Say what they get | 1 promise, in their terms. Smaller than your proof. |
| 4 | Mechanism | Give a reason to believe | Feature, which means promise, which is why it works. No product name yet. |
| 5 | Objection | Handle what they're thinking | Use the failed solutions and objections from the customer model |
| 6 | Proof | Back the claims | Only real proof from the brief, or a placeholder |
| 7 | Offer | Present the product and terms | Frame the price. State the effort. |
| 8 | Risk reversal | Make yes feel safe | Specific guarantee |
| 9 | Call to action | Tell them what to do | Action, what happens next, effort, a reason for now |

Then close, where it fits, on an **identity line** tied to something the product really does: "The people who cook on cast iron aren't better cooks. They just stopped replacing pans."

Beat details that matter most:

- **Problem.** Escalate toward 1 consequence. If any 2 lines could swap places and nothing changes, you've written a list. Frame it as an observation, never pity. "You've stopped searing steak" is a fact. "Are you suffering with ruined pans?" is a pity frame that brand teams hate.
- **Promise before mechanism.** People drop off through an ad. Lead with what they get, which everyone understands, then explain why. "You'll sear a steak tonight like a restaurant, because cast iron holds heat when the cold meat hits it."
- **Mechanism.** Every physical product has one, because someone made a choice about material, process or design. Find it in the product facts. A contradiction ("non-stick without coating") isn't a mechanism until you say why.
- **Objections, in this order:** this isn't for me, this won't work, it won't work for me, it's not worth it, what if I'm wrong, I'll do it later.
- **Proof ladder, strongest first:** demonstration on camera, third-party verification, volume of reviews or customers, named testimony, mechanism explanation, founder assertion. A demo needs no customers, so new brands should lean on it. Proof must feel bigger than the claim.
- **CTA.** "Shop now" names an action and nothing else. Better: "Tap the link, pick your size, it ships tomorrow. The launch price ends Sunday." Only give a deadline the brand actually has.

### Step 6: write the hooks

Write 5 to 10 hooks. Each must flow straight into line 2 of your body, so read every hook together with line 2 before keeping it.

The hook's job is relevance, not shock. It tells the right person "this is about you" and quietly filters out everyone else. The platform shows the ad to people like those who keep watching, so a clickbait hook attracts the wrong crowd.

- Make it feel like content, not an ad. Polished packshots, a centred logo and "Introducing..." read as advertising and get skipped. A person mid-thought, a phone screenshot or a plain statement reads as content.
- Use the customer's words from step 1.
- Name the problem, not a demographic. "If you've stopped searing steak at home" beats "Home cooks, listen up."
- The most specific detail can do 3 jobs at once: target, prove and cast.
- First frame, spoken line, on-screen text and caption should all point at the same 1 claim.

Rotate hook types across your variants:

| Type | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Accommodation | You've stopped [thing they gave up]. You didn't decide to. |
| Trigger moment | The day [moment] happens is the day you [realise]. |
| Contrarian | [Common belief] is why [problem]. |
| Curiosity gap | Why [surprising fact about the problem]. |
| Failed solution | You've tried [common fix]. Here's why it didn't stick. |
| Truth bomb | Yes, it's [price]. Here's why people still buy it. |
| Confrontation | That [object they own and regret]? That's the problem. |
| Specific proof | (Only with a real number from the brief) |
| Sensory | No words. 2 to 3 seconds of texture, sound or a satisfying action. |

For each hook give the spoken line, the on-screen text and the first frame.

### Step 7: bridges, feeling and voice

**Bridges.** Most viewers leave at the joins. Check 2 of them:

- Hook into body: the hook must set up line 2, not just sit on top of it.
- Product reveal: let product awareness rise gradually. Don't jump from a relatable story to "That's why [Brand] is your solution." Show the product on screen first and say its name later, often in the offer.

**Feeling.** Ask "so what?" after each line. If the only answer is a feature, add what it means for the person. People buy a better version of themselves, so tie features to identity: "holds heat" becomes "the person who finally gets a crust on a steak."

**Voice.** Scripts are heard, not read.

- 1 idea per sentence. Verbs early. Plain words.
- Contractions always. "Cannot" sounds like a robot.
- Vary sentence length. All short, choppy lines read as AI. Try long, long, short.
- Grade 7 reading level or below unless the audience is expert.
- Avoid AI tells: em dashes, "it's not X, it's Y" chains, stacked rhetorical questions, "game-changer", "elevate", "seamless".
- Match the brand voice from the brief.
- Read it aloud in your head at speaking pace. Split anything that runs out of breath.

**Who's speaking.** A hired UGC creator should only say "I" about experiences they've actually had with the product. Write their lines as things they can honestly say after using it, or switch to 2nd person, or attribute the story ("A customer told us..."). Founders can tell the founding story. Avatars and claymation characters are fiction and should read as such: a generated presenter tells the problem and shows the product, and gives no personal testimonial.

**A picture that can be made.** Each line's visual is something that can be filmed or generated: the product as it really looks, an adult, a hand, a place, a simple drawing. Not a celebrity, a real person who has not been filmed, a real news clip, a child, or words to be read inside the video (on-screen text is added afterwards).

### Step 8: self-audit

Check before returning. Count words exactly with code if you can run it, and report that number, not an estimate. Estimates drift, and a "60 second" script that's really 75 seconds gets cut badly in editing. Aim for 90 to 100% of the target word count: it is set from the speaker's pace, which is in the length guide below. Don't say the same price or line twice to fill time.

Then list every number in the script and trace each one to the brief. Only state that something was checked (numbers, overlap, compliance) if you actually checked it. If a check was a judgement call, say so.

- [ ] Awareness stage stated. Customer model shown and marked as assumed.
- [ ] Hook uses the customer's words, reads as content, and flows into line 2
- [ ] Problem names an accommodation or consequence, speaks to 1 person, doesn't resolve
- [ ] 1 promise, before the mechanism (a 1st person story may imply it)
- [ ] Mechanism uses real product facts. Product not named before the mechanism.
- [ ] Objections handled. Proof is real or a flagged placeholder.
- [ ] Price framed, effort stated, guarantee specific (if the brand has one)
- [ ] CTA has action, next step, effort and a real reason for now
- [ ] Closes on identity tied to a real product fact, or says why not
- [ ] Spoken word count fits the length at the speaker's pace (the length guide)
- [ ] Every number traced to the brief. No invented reviews, quotes, awards, stories or small "real life" specifics. No unsupported comparisons. No claim the brief rules out.
- [ ] Contractions, varied sentences, no AI tells, brand voice matched
- [ ] Reference remix shares no distinctive 6+ word phrase with the reference

### Step 9: recommend a format

Choose format after the script. Find the beat doing the most persuading:

| Load-bearing beat | Suits |
|---|---|
| Mechanism (the benefit is invisible) | Animation, claymation, faceless educational, VSL, UGC that cuts to animation |
| Proof (the result is visible) | UGC demo, founder demo, side-by-side test |
| Offer (the terms are the edge) | Short UGC, or a static or carousel |
| Problem (a story with stakes) | UGC story, founder story, VSL |

## Length guide

The pace follows the speaker's age, in spoken words a second: 3.6 under 30, 3.4 for 30 to 44, 3.2 for 45 to 59, 3.0 for 60 to 74, 2.8 from 75, and 3.3 when the age is not known. A calm pace is a sixth slower. The app works out the words for a length from this 1 table (`src/lib/pace.ts`) and gives them with the brief.

| Beat | 15s | 30s | 60s | 1 to 3 min | 5 min+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | 1 line | 1 line | 1 line | 1 line | 1 line |
| Problem | 1 line | 1 line | 1 to 2 lines | Several lines | A scene |
| Promise | Merged with offer | 1 line | 1 to 2 lines | 2 lines | 2 lines |
| Mechanism | None | 0 to 1 line | 1 to 2 lines | Can be most of the ad | Large, repeated |
| Objection | None | None | 1 to 2 lines | Several | Several blocks |
| Proof | None | 1 short | 1 piece | 1 to 3 pieces | 5 or more |
| Offer | Short | Short | Short | Short | Short |
| Risk reversal | None | None | 1 line | 1 line | 1 line |
| CTA | 1 line | 1 line | 1 line | 2 to 3 lines | Several, spaced out |

Long ads loop: problem, promise, mechanism, objection, proof, then a new problem, up to 3 times. A timeline works well (day 1, week 2, month 3). Write each length from scratch; a 5 minute script trimmed to 60 seconds keeps the wrong bridges.

## Story scripts

Story isn't a beat. It carries all 9 beats inside a narrative, and viewers stop judging the ad while they wait to find out what happened. Use stories for 45 seconds and up.

| Shape | Carries | Opening pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Failed-solutions journey | Objection | I tried [A], then [B], then [C]. |
| Discovery | Mechanism | I found out why [problem] happens, and it changed what I bought. |
| Transformation with a witness | Proof | [Someone close] noticed before I did. |
| Confession | Problem | I've been [accommodation] for a year. I never told anyone. |

A story needs stakes: something risked, lost or embarrassing. 1st person past tense usually performs best. Founder or customer stories must be real. Invented ones belong to fictional characters (animation, claymation) and should read as fiction.

## What this reads from the brand brain

By design, little: this skill is for a brand whose brain is thin. What it uses when it is there, and what it does when it is not:

| Need | Document and field | When it is blank |
|---|---|---|
| The product and how it works | `products-and-positioning.md`, per product: What it is, Key features, How it is used | Ask. This is the 1 thing that cannot be assumed. |
| The buyer | `customer-avatars.md`: Who they are, Why they buy this product, What almost stops them | Build the assumed customer model (step 1) and mark it `Assumed customer model`. |
| Price, offer, guarantee | `offers.json`; `brand.json`: guarantee; `products-and-positioning.md`: Pricing summary | Placeholders such as `[PRICE]`; no guarantee is stated. |
| Proof | `brand.json`: claims with sources, proof assets | A demonstration, or a flagged placeholder. Nothing is made up. |
| Voice | `brand-guidelines.md`: Brand voice and tone | Plain spoken English. |

## Example

Brief: Ferrous, a fictional pre-seasoned cast iron skillet. $129. Lifetime warranty. Made in Ballarat. ICP: home cooks 28 to 40 who have replaced non-stick pans before. Voice: dry, confident. 45 seconds, founder to camera. Problem aware.

Assumed customer model: wants to cook like they know what they're doing; has stopped searing at high heat; tried cheap non-stick sets that peeled; trigger is coating flakes in food; objections are weight, fuss, price; says "my pans", "sticks", "peeling".

| Beat | Spoken line |
|---|---|
| Hook | You've stopped cooking steak at home. You didn't decide to. Your pan decided for you. |
| Problem | Non-stick can't take real heat, so you cook everything on medium. |
| Promise | I make a pan you can cook on at full heat for the rest of your life. |
| Mechanism | It's cast iron, pre-seasoned in our Ballarat foundry, so there's no coating to peel. The more you cook, the slicker it gets. |
| Objection | Yes, it's heavier. That weight holds the heat when the cold steak hits it. |
| Offer | It's $129, and it comes with a lifetime warranty. Rinse, dry, a wipe of oil. That's the care routine. |
| CTA | Link's below. Buy it once. |
| Identity | People who cook on cast iron aren't better cooks. They just stopped buying new pans. |

Spoken words: 118 (about 45 seconds). No reviews or numbers were invented. The "slicker over time" line assumes normal seasoning behaviour, and the brand should confirm it.
