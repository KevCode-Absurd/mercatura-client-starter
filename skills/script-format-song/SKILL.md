# Script format: song ad

Load this with a script task skill. Where the two differ, this wins.

A song ad is judged on 2 things: it is a real song a stranger would hear out, and its catchiest line lands the customer's outcome in the first 5 seconds.

## What this surface carries

A micro music video. The song is the spine: it sets the pace, the mood and the arc, and the pictures ride on it. It carries a feeling and a line that stays; it is weak for a mechanism, proof and offer terms.

By stage: strongest for problem-aware and solution-aware buyers (the verse is life before, the chorus the outcome). It works for unaware buyers when verse 1 is their own moment. Weak for a buyer who only needs the offer.

## The lyric is the script

- **1 lyric line is 1 script line,** 6 to 9 sung words. What is said on that line is the sung words and nothing else.
- **The visual note says how the line is shown.** `SUNG` when the lead sings it on camera; otherwise the silent picture cut to the music, showing what the line says.
- **Section tags go in the visual note,** in square brackets, on the first line of each section: `[Verse 1]`, `[Chorus]`, `[Bridge]`. They are not sung and do not count.
- **The style line for the music tool opens the first line's visual note:** `Style:` then under 25 words: genre, vocal (a female lead, a male rap, a choir), tempo, mood, production (modern and polished, lo-fi, vintage). Never an artist's or a band's name: describe the sound.
- Line 1, for a hiking boot. Said: "Mud on the trail and your feet stay dry". Visual note: "Style: indie folk, warm female lead, 96 BPM, hopeful, acoustic. [Verse 1] SUNG, lacing a boot on a porch step."
- On-screen text: only the offer and the call to action, on the last line.

## Lyric rules

- **Hook in the first 5 seconds:** the catchiest line is in the first 12 sung words. Vocals start at once: no instrumental intro, no breaks, a tight ending.
- **The lyric carries the angle, not the brand.** Sing the customer's outcome. The product's name is not the hook.
- **A real song, never a jingle:** real verses, a real chorus, a real rhyme scheme.
- **Short visual lines:** each names something that can be seen and fits a shot of 2.2 to 3.5 seconds.
- **Who sings.** The lead sings to "you", or as a character in a story. It is fiction: it never sings a result of its own as fact.

## Structure by length

A song runs at about 2.5 sung words a second. Write to the length given.

- About 40 sung words, 15 seconds: the hook and 1 pass of the chorus.
- About 75, 30 seconds: verse, chorus, verse, chorus.
- About 150, 60 seconds: a full song with a bridge.

The song is made afterwards in a music tool, once and whole, from the lyric and the style line. Its real timings then set the cut. The picture is generated in clips of about 10 seconds: 3 lyric lines to a clip, and no line runs across a boundary.

## The lead and the pictures

- **1 recurring lead:** a stylised animated character or object. A fictional adult by proportions and dress; never a child, a real person or a known character.
- **The lead rarely sings all the way through.** Mark `SUNG` where a face earns it: the hook and the last chorus at least. The rest is silent picture.
- **Pictures must be producible as animated shots:** animated objects, a stylised lead, simple shapes, 1 action. No live-action crane shots.
- 1 place holds everything the lyric names, from the first shot. The lead turns to the lens to sing and back to the action.
- No words inside the picture; the product is the real one.

## What a sung line may claim

A sung line claims as much as a spoken one. A number, a time, a health result or a named competitor comes only from the brand's facts. If the fact does not fit the metre, change the line, never the fact. Plain exaggeration and metaphor are free. A picture shows only what its line states.

## What to avoid

- The brand's name as the chorus. A list of features set to music. A lead who sings every line.
