# Script surfaces

The script comes first. Only when the argument exists do you ask what the ad looks like. A surface is how the script shows up: a person talking to camera, an animation, a long-copy static, a designed static, a carousel.

Most format failures are writing failures that look like production failures: a beat was put on a surface that can't hold it. No surface beats the others in general. What matters is the fit, and the reading is judged on it: every "must" points at a line of the script that would fail without it.

## Step 1: find the load-bearing beat

In every script 1 beat does most of the persuading. Which one is a property of the proposition, not of the format.

| The product's edge | Load-bearing beat | Typical of |
|---|---|---|
| Invisible: it works where nobody can see, or only over years | Mechanism | An active in a serum, a supplement; how long a pan or a jacket will last; anything whose difference is in how it is made |
| Visible: you can watch it work | Proof | A tool, a fabric shedding water, a texture, a pour, a change the brand can film |
| In the terms | Offer | A price, a trial, a warranty, a bundle nobody else gives |
| In a shared experience | Problem | Products bought to end a daily compromise |

Read the script, not the product page: the beat where the script spends its time and its best sentences is load-bearing, whatever the brief said. The same product gives scripts with different load-bearing beats: a jacket shown under a hose rests on proof; the same jacket argued from its seams rests on mechanism.

## Step 2: say what the script needs from a surface

6 properties. For each, decide whether this script must have it, is helped by it, or doesn't need it, and say why in terms of the script's own lines.

| Property | The question | Beats that depend on it |
|---|---|---|
| A believed human face | Does a beat only work if a real-looking person is seen living it or vouching for it? | Problem, proof |
| Shows what a camera cannot | Does a beat describe something no camera can film (inside the body, inside a material, a process over weeks or years, a comparison of invisible things)? | Mechanism, sometimes proof |
| Order is controlled | Does the argument only work if it is received in sequence (a promise that pays off, a reveal, a story)? | Promise through to the call to action |
| Sound can be assumed | Is the script written to be heard: a voice, a tone, a pause? | Every spoken beat |
| Holds attention long enough | Does a beat need time to develop: a problem scene, a mechanism taught in steps, objections answered? | Problem, mechanism, offer |
| Reads as a person, not an advertisement | Does the opening only work if it sounds like someone talking, not a brand? | Hook, problem |

A first-person story can't open on a promise, so it does not need the promise early; it needs order and a voice. A script with 1 line and an offer needs none of these: it needs a clear frame.

A believed face is not a real customer. A generated presenter can live the problem on camera and cannot vouch for a result: proof only a real person can give needs that person's own footage.

## Step 3: what each surface holds

The app scores the surfaces from your answers. This is what it scores against, so that your reasons and any blend are in the same terms.

- **1 person to camera** (UGC, founder, expert): a believed face, a controlled order, sound. It can't show what a camera cannot, and holds attention poorly alone.
- **Drawn, rendered or animated** (claymation, cartoon, comic, explainer, a talking object): shows the unshowable, controls the order, holds attention. No believed face.
- **Mixed video** (a person, then animation, then the person; a VSL): everything, at the cost of 2 kinds of footage.
- **Long-form native static**: holds a reader and reads as a person. No face, no sound, no controlled order.
- **Designed static**: a glance. A diagram at most. **Carousel**: a partial order, a diagram a card.

So a script resting on proof fails with no believable face; one resting on an invisible mechanism fails as a talking head; one that needs its order fails as a static; one written to be heard fails with the sound off unless every line is also on screen. When 2 beats need different things, a video can change surface part-way: that is what `blend` is for.

## Step 4: length

No length is best. Longer ads cost more to make and tend to scale further, because length is what an unaware buyer needs. How each beat changes with length:

| Beat | About 15 s | 30 to 60 s | 1 to 3 min | 5 min and over |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | 1 line | 1 line | 1 line | 1 line |
| Problem | 1 line | 1 to 2 lines | Several lines | A scene of 2 to 3 minutes |
| Promise | None | 1 sentence | 1 to 2 sentences | 1 to 2 sentences |
| Mechanism | None | 1 sentence | 2 to 4 sentences; can be most of the ad | A major section, taught and kept entertaining |
| Objection | None | 1 line | 2 to 3, several objections | Large blocks, often inside the problem scene |
| Proof | None or 1 | 1 | 1 to 3 pieces | 5 or 6 pieces, every rung of the proof ladder |
| Offer | Short | Short | Short | Short |
| Risk reversal | None | Rarely | 1 line | 1 line each time |
| Call to action | 1 line | 1 line | 2 to 3 lines | Several, at intervals |

- The hook and the offer never grow. Length comes from the problem, the mechanism, objections and proof.
- A script whose load-bearing beat has no room at its length is the wrong length. A mechanism-led script at 15 seconds has nowhere to put the mechanism. A demonstration is the exception: shown, it is hook and proof at once, and fits 15 seconds.
- Long ads loop: problem, promise, mechanism, objection, proof, then a second problem and round again, usually 3 times, with a call to action after each loop. A common vehicle is a timeline (the first weeks of using something, or the life of a thing that is kept), each step with its own problem solved and its own mechanism.
- Each length is its own script. A long script cut down by an editor makes a poor short ad: its bridges were built for the long version.

## What to return

- The load-bearing beat, the kind of edge, and 1 or 2 sentences on why, quoting the script.
- For each of the 6 properties: must, helps or no, with the reason in terms of this script's lines.
- Whether the script fits its length, the length it is best made at, and where to add length if a longer version is tested.
- If 2 beats need different surfaces, how to blend them in 1 video.

Do not lean toward a format because it is fashionable, or because the category usually uses it.

## What this reads from the brand brain

Only what the app sends with the script: the category and each product's name and format (`brand.json`), the buyer's summary (`customer-avatars.md`), the concept's angle and stage. Where the format is blank, take the kind of edge from the script's own lines and say so in `why`.
