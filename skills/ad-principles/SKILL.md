# What makes an ad work

An ad takes 1 buyer from where they are now, a state they do not want, to where they want to be, and the product is the bridge between the two. It is told the way a person talks to a friend. There is no fixed order and there are no required parts.

The order of priority for every word comes first with this request: natural, then it sells, then it tells a story, then the brand's language, and the brand's devices last and optional. Everything below sits inside that order. Where a line here and that order disagree, the order wins.

## Say it like a person

Read every line aloud: if a person would not say it to a friend, say it the plain way. A long sentence joined by "and", "so" or "because" is how people talk. A clipped one is a failure: not "Doctor said more vegetables. I nodded and grabbed a burger." but "My doctor told me to eat more vegetables. I nodded, and then I went and got a burger." (The examples are made up. Take no fact from one.)

3 things the order of priority does not say:

- **A customer's words are vocabulary to borrow.** Say the thing in the speaker's own sentence, with their word for it. Do not recite a review or the brief.
- **No stock turn.** Not "here's the thing", "here's the part nobody tells you", "and that's when", a number announced before it is said, "it's not X, it's Y" (in 1 sentence or across 2), a row of "no this, no that", a list of three as a habit, a question you answer yourself, a mirrored saying of 2 matching halves, or a slogan as the last line. Get to the next thought with a plain joining word.
- **The logic holds.** Every line makes sense on first hearing, and a normal person would do, think or feel this.

**Technical terms follow the buyer's stage.** The rule for this buyer comes with the request. In short: a buyer who does not know the answer yet has no word for what is in the product, so there are none, and what a thing does is said in the words they use for their own life; a buyer comparing products is helped by the category's own terms, each said beside what it does for them. A fact that is worded technically is said plainly where the buyer needs it plain: the fact is held, its wording is not.

## One thread, and every sentence leads to the next

Before the lines, decide how this ad is told, in 1 sentence: who is speaking, about what moment, and where the product comes in. Everything belongs to that telling. Each sentence is there to get the next one heard, joined the way talk is joined: "so", "but", "which is why", "and that's when". The listener is never left to connect 2 things alone, and should never feel the moment the selling starts.

### How a story moves

Where a story fits, and it nearly always fits a buyer who does not yet know the product, it moves like this, at whatever length the stage gives it:

- **Before.** Their life with the problem: 1 person, small exact scenes, what they do to live with it, in the customer's own words. Stay until it is felt. A long story has several such moments, each leading into the next.
- **What they tried.** The usual fixes, and what each one cost or why they gave up. This is the bridge most ads skip.
- **The turn.** How they came across the product, in a way that would really happen (someone gave it to them, they gave in to an ad), with their doubt said aloud. Or 1 plain idea of why nothing else worked.
- **The product, as the bridge.** What it is and the first time using it, small and specific. A fact comes in because the story needs it at that moment, with what it means for them. A fact or a figure is never set down on its own.
- **After.** The storyteller's own ordinary morning since: what they stopped doing, what is easier now, as 1 small picture. Only the kind of thing real customers report. The story ends with them, never with a survey or with what "people say". This is what the buyer is buying, so show it.
- **The next step,** said the way a friend would say it. An ad always keeps one.

### A buyer who already knows the product

They need less of this: the 1 doubt that stops them, answered fully with the proof that fits that doubt, then what makes trying it safe. Stay on that doubt. Do not teach the problem or sell the product to them again. A short true moment still beats a list of points.

## The reason for the ad

The request gives the reason for this one: who it is for, what they want, what they do now, what they tried, what stops them, their words, the offer. Write from that.

- Use the customer's word for the problem, not the category's.
- Nothing invented: no customer, quote, number, study, award or deadline that was not sent. A figure you need and do not have is a [placeholder]. A scene, a feeling, an exaggeration and a metaphor are yours.
- Proof backs the claim this ad makes, not another one: a figure for how many people keep using something proves it gets used, not that it works. A story and a statistic do not sit side by side: choose the one this buyer believes.
- Check feature, mechanism and benefit separately: what it contains, how it produces an effect, and what the buyer gets. A verified material or ingredient does not prove a performance result. Explain only supported links in the buyer's language; no mandatory feature-first order.
- What 1 customer said is what 1 customer said. It never becomes "people say" or "most people".
- The next step is one this buyer can take today.
- Answer the doubts this buyer has, and no others.
- 1 idea an ad.
- The deal is 1 sentence a person would say across a table: what it costs to start and the 1 term that makes trying it safe. Never a list of terms. For a buyer who does not know the product yet, little or none of it.

Visual notes give the real product a feasible place in the scene: who holds or wears it, what supports it and the verified use shown. Size, fit and packaging are product facts, not creative exaggeration. Product presence follows the approved concept; camera and framing can give it prominence. [Product placement](../production-principles/references/product-placement.md) carries that handoff into references, scale and physical integration, without adding a new claim or changing the approved copy.

The [commercial treatment](../production-principles/references/commercial-image-treatments.md) follows the concept: casual UGC, controlled luxury, seasonal context or vertical composition can carry the same supported argument. Before/after and implied post-use improvement are result claims, even without text; they need approved evidence, not a convincing generated face. A season supplies context, never an invented offer or benefit.

## The brand's voice

A shared frustration can supply an angle when research supports it: an industry practice, an unhelpful belief or the usual way of doing the job. Name the frustration and the supported difference this product makes. Do not invent a villain, accuse a competitor, shame the buyer or equate unfamiliar ingredients with harm. This is optional, not a requirement for every ad.

The brand's words for its product, its attitude, who it talks to and what it never says all apply. In a story or in spoken copy the speaker is a person, not the brand: slogans and headline rhythm stay out of their mouth. A device the brand sometimes uses (a rhythm of 3, very short sentences) is a suggestion for a line that takes it without bending, never a rule for every line.

For spoken ads, words, voice identity and performance are separate decisions. A stable voice can shift its energy with the story without becoming a different speaker. Natural phrasing and complete words come first; pauses, emphasis and any emotional progression fit the requested pace and duration. Keep spoken text clean. Useful production cues belong in the task's existing direction/notes fields, not bracket tags or invented personal experience inside the copy. [Voice identity and spoken performance](../production-principles/references/voice-performance.md) explains the handoff to native video or an optional supported speech route; it does not prescribe a new structure or four script variants.

## Length

As long as this buyer needs. A buyer who does not know the problem needs the most: the ad is the story of the problem, told properly, and it may run very long. A buyer who knows the product needs the least. The request gives the range for this buyer's stage, and that range wins over a length in the format notes and over the length of the brand's own ads. Inside it, never squeeze a story to fit and never pad one: an ad is long because more happens in it.
