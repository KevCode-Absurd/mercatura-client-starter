# Format: cinematic 3D

Where this and the task skill differ, this wins.

Cinematic 3D is a look. The story comes from the script; this says what the look adds and where it is weak.

## What makes it cinematic 3D

- **Photographic light and materials.** Light behaves as in life: real reflections, fabric weave, wood grain, glass, liquid. The room and the product look touchable.
- **Designed, animated forms.** Every shape is that of an animated feature film. It is plainly animation, never live action, and that is what lets it show what a camera cannot.
- **Believable adults.** About 6 heads tall, with a refined face (cheekbones, jaw, brow) and a simplified nose. Not a toy, not a cartoon.
- **The eye.** Slightly enlarged and expressive, with detailed, realistic irises: enough to read as animated, never cartoon-big. A feeling is read on the face.
- **A film finish.** Film colour grading and shallow focus, each frame composed like a still from a film.

There is 1 look: preset `default`.

## What it is good for

It has a face that acts, so it carries **feeling**: a problem lived by 1 person in a room the buyer knows. It carries a **mechanism** in a close view with no people, and exteriors, home scenes and the product alone. A character may speak on camera.

It is weak for proof that rests on a real person: its people are fiction. A product alone reads as a product photograph, so the look adds nothing there unless a character or a stylised prop shares the frame.

By stage: strongest for problem-aware buyers (the feeling acted on a face) and solution-aware buyers (the mechanism shown mid-action); it reaches unaware buyers as a small film story. A product-aware buyer gets an accurate product but needs proof from elsewhere. For a buyer who only needs the offer, another format does more.

## A picture claims as much as a sentence

- Show only what the script's lines and the brand's claims state. A mechanism is shown only as `How it works` gives it, in plain visual words (layers, strands, a droplet). No body before and after. A metaphor stays plainly a metaphor. Numbers, study results and medical results come only from the brand's facts.
- Every character is an original, fictional adult, plain from proportions and dress. Never a child, a real person or a known character, and no studio's name or logo. A character is never a customer, a founder or an expert and gives no testimonial, even when it speaks.
- The product is accurate: its true shape and colours, every line of its label copy given exactly, with its colour.

## What to avoid

- A character who tells the viewer what the product did for them.
- A feeling written as an adjective: it renders as a pleasant blank face.
- 2 subjects or 2 actions in 1 frame.
- A film scene with no product in it until an advert starts at the end.

## What this reads from the brand brain

- `customer-avatars.md`: `Who they are` (the character's age band, look and dress), `The moment` (the scene), `What almost stops them`, `Tried before`, `Customer language bank` (the lines). Blank: ask, or mark the story inferred.
- `products-and-positioning.md`: `Looks like` and `Pack` (shape, colours, label copy), `How it is used`, `Visible result`, `How it works`. Blank `How it works`: ask; never invent a mechanism.
- `brand-guidelines.md`: `Brand voice and tone` (the narrator or the speaker), `Brand world` (places, light), `Color palette`, `Typography` (words on a still), `Brand guardrails`.
- `brand.json` and `offers.json`: claims, forbidden claims, the offer, the guarantee.
